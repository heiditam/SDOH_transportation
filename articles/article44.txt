Abstract
Introduction
Although a homebound state in late life is often a result of cognitive and/or physical/functional health problems, transportation barriers may also be an important contributor. In this study, we examined the associations of homebound states with driving status and self-reported transportation barriers to social/community activities.
Methods
We used the 2023 National Health and Aging Trends Study (N = 7543), and homebound older adults were defined as those who rarely (≤1 a week) or never went outside the home in the past month. We used generalized linear models to examine the associations.
Results
Homebound older adults comprised 5.2% of Medicare beneficiaries age 65 and older. Only 27% reported driving in the past month, and 54.2% and 32.1% reported health-related and transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities, respectively. Multivariable analysis results showed that homebound older adults were more likely to have stopped driving in the past year than their peers who went out 2–4 days a week (IRR = 2.28, 95% CI = 1.52–3.43) and those who went out 5+ days a week (IRR = 7.20, 95% CI = 4.34–11.97). Homebound older adults were also more likely to report transportation barriers than those who went out 2–4 days a week (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.06–1.58) and those who went out 5+ days a week (IRR = 1.99, 95% CI = 1.50–2.65). Less than 10% of homebound older adults used taxi/ride-hailing services and public transportation.
Conclusion
The study suggests that older adults' homebound state significantly correlates with transportation barriers. Reliable transportation services appropriate to older adults’ capabilities are needed to facilitate their participation in social/community activities.

1. Introduction
Homebound older adults comprise a significant share of the 65 and older age group in the U.S. A study of Medicare beneficiaries between 2011 and 2017 found that 8.3% were chronically homebound (i.e., never/rarely went outside the home in the past month), and 26.2% became homebound over the 7-year study period (Xiang et al., 2020). Another study of Medicare beneficiaries showed that 16.2% were homebound or semi-homebound (i.e., only left the home with assistance or had difficulty or needed help leaving the house) in 2019 (Ankuda et al., 2022). Compared to their non-homebound peers, homebound older adults report significantly higher levels of social isolation and loneliness, which, in turn, can increase the risk of homebound progression (Cudjoe et al., 2022; Ezeokonkwo et al., 2021; Garabrant and Liu, 2021; Yang et al., 2021).
Homebound state is often a result of underlying chronic medical conditions, functional and cognitive disabilities, depression/anxiety, and is also associated with older age, female sex, racial/ethnic minority status, not having a college degree, and low income (Ankuda et al., 2021, 2022; Ornstein et al., 2015, 2020; Musich et al., 2015; Xiang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). However, social isolation and loneliness in homebound older adults stem from not only health-related barriers but also transportation-related barriers as they reduce or cease to drive due to physical, psychological, and other factors associated with driving safety (Ang et al., 2019; Maliheh et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2020; Wood et al., 2023). Given the car-centric life in the U.S., driving reduction or cessation poses significant barriers to mobility and participation in social and community activities that play an important role in the health, well-being, and quality of life of older adults as those activities provide social interactions and a sense of social connectedness, a key determinant of healthy aging (Cheng et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2020; Ho et al., 2023; Kuspinar et al., 2020; Mehrabi and Béland, 2020).
Mobility is essential for keeping older adults active, socially connected, and independent (Umstattd Meyer et al., 2014). Since mobility in late life encompasses not only physical movement but also the ability to engage socially and participate in community life, it has been referred to as life-space mobility or the ability to move within environments that expand from one's home to the greater community (Baker et al., 2003; Johnson et al., 2020; Peel et al., 2005). Life-space mobility is determined by social and environmental, as well as physical and functional, factors that affect how people live their everyday lives (Dunlap et al., 2022; Mauldin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Research has shown that declined and restricted life-space mobility in older adults is associated with greater risks of depression (Polku et al., 2015), incident falls and fractures (Lo et al., 2014), emergency department visits, hospitalization, skilled nursing facility stay, and increased total healthcare costs (Kennedy et al., 2019; Sheets et al., 2021), and mortality (Kennedy et al., 2017).
With the broad definition of mobility that expands beyond one's home, transportation becomes an important factor as vehicles and other forms of transportation are required for older adults to maintain access to essential services, activities, and people (Dickerson et al., 2007, 2019; Oxley and Whelan, 2008; Webber et al., 2010). A longitudinal study found that the use of alternative transportation (getting a ride, public and paratransit transportation, or walking/using a wheelchair/scooter) among both driving and nondriving older adults, bolstered by higher levels of neighborhood social cohesion, was associated with participating in more diverse types of social activities and less steep declines in social participation over time (Latham-Mintus et al., 2022). However, many older adults, especially homebound older adults, face challenges accessing reliable transportation for social and community activity participation.
A large proportion of non-driving older adults rely on rides from family and friends (Choi and DiNitto, 2016); however, they often do not want to burden family members and friends/neighbors, except for essential activities such as healthcare appointments (Adler and Rottunda, 2011; Mullen et al., 2017). Some older adults do not have family or friends or do not have them always available (Mullen et al., 2017). Public transportation or paratransit services for older adults are limited or non-existent, especially in nonurban areas (Bezyak et al., 2020; Desai et al., 2024; Gleason and Silverstein, 2023; Remillard et al., 2022). Older adults did not always consider public transportation convenient and safe (Adler and Rottunda, 2011). Public transportation services may also be out of reach for those who use mobility assistive devices and must get to a station not a short distance away. A study based on the 2011 National Health and Aging Trends Study found that about one in ten urban-dwelling older adults in the U.S., who are more likely to be non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic than non-Hispanic White, reported use of public transportation in the last month, and over 20% of users relied on it to see their regular doctors (Gimie et al., 2022). However, frailty and living in an area with cracked or broken sidewalks were associated with lower odds of public transportation use (Gimie et al., 2022). As ride-hailing services like Uber and Lyft become popular, many older adults, especially those who are technology-savvy and desire to remain independent, use the services for their convenience and dependability, but affordability among low-income older adults remains a barrier (Bayne et al., 2021; Misra et al., 2022).
Transportation challenges for older adults with physical and functional mobility impairments are a critical life-space mobility issue (Viljanen et al., 2016). Homebound older adults who do not drive still want to engage in social and community activities. A study found that despite functional limitations, depression, pain, and falls, homebound older adults reported that activities outside the home—visiting family/friends, attending religious services, participating in meetings/clubs, and going out for enjoyment—were important to them; however, nearly one out of five listed transportation as a barrier to these activities (Szanton et al., 2016). Interventions to improve these older adults’ life-space mobility, including improved access to transportation and age-friendly infrastructure, must be a significant public health priority in a rapidly aging society (Levasseur et al., 2015).
1.1. Study aims, theoretical framework, and hypotheses
While previous research on life-space mobility among older adults has provided valuable insights into the importance of transportation for participation in social/community activities, studies focused on homebound older adults have been lacking. In the present study, using a nationally representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries age 65 and older, we first examined the associations of homebound states with driving status and self-reported transportation barriers to engaging in social/community activities. Second, we examined the associations of self-reported transportation barriers with alternative transportation use among non-drivers, including homebound non-drivers.
For the study's conceptual framework, we adopted the socioecological mobility theory that links multiple determinants of mobility via “walking, wheeling, driving, and taking alternate forms of transportation” (Webber et al., 2010, p. 445) and illustrates how mobility impairments in late life can lead to limitations in accessing different life spaces. Specifically, the socioecological mobility theory posits that mobility is determined by cognitive (e.g., executive function and memory), physical/functional, psychosocial (e.g., depression, coping behaviors, and relationship with others), and financial factors intersecting with gender and cultural and biographical influences. Later studies found that physical and psychosocial health tends to be consistently significant determinants, while the significance of other factors varied depending on the study sample (Giannouli et al., 2019; Umstattd Meyer et al., 2014). In this study, we posited that homebound state was partly determined by non-driving and transportation barriers to social/community activities, along with sociodemographic factors and cognitive, physical/functional, and psychosocial health. Study hypotheses were: controlling for sociodemographic factors and cognitive, physical/functional, and psychosocial health, (H1) non-driving status and (H2) self-reported transportation barriers would be significantly associated with homebound than non-homebound status; and (H3) among all and homebound non-driving older adults, self-reported transportation barriers would be negatively associated with the use of alternative transportation. The findings of this study provide valuable insights into transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities among homebound older adults.
2. Methods
2.1. Data source
Data came from the 2023 U.S. National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS). NHATS collects data annually from a nationally representative panel of Medicare beneficiaries age 65+ on their physical, functional, cognitive, and sensory capacity, social, physical, and technological environments, and participation in valued activities. The initial NHATS sample persons were interviewed in 2011, and the first, second, and third replenishment samples were added in 2015, 2022, and 2023, respectively (Freedman et al., 2024). The 2023 NHATS data were collected in in-person interviews. In this study, we focused on 7543 sample persons, representing approximately 46.4 million Medicare beneficiaries age 65+, who were living in their own homes or residential care communities (but not in nursing homes) and self-reported data (i.e., no proxy interview). We excluded proxy-interviewed sample persons (n = 390) to ensure that all data were self-reported. Based on the analysis of de-identified public-use data, this study was exempt from the authors’ institutional review board review.
2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Homebound versus non-homebound older adults
NHATS respondents were asked, “In the last month, how often did you leave your building/home to go outside?” Response categories were never, rarely (once a week or less), some days (2–4 days a week), most days (5–6 days a week), and every day (7 days a week). Following previous NHATS-based studies (Ankuda et al., 2021, 2022; Ornstein et al., 2015, 2020; Xiang et al., 2020), we defined homebound older adults as those who never or rarely left home in the past month as opposed to non-homebound older adults who went out at least 2 days a week. Those who were not homebound were divided into two groups to examine further variations: those who went out 2–4 days a week and those who went out 5+ days a week.
2.2.2. Past-month social/community activities and health-related and transportation barriers to participation
These activities included the following: (a) visits with family/friends; (b) attendance in religious services (either in person or online); (c) participation in clubs, classes, or other organized activities than religious services (either in person or online; referred to as attendance in clubs/meetings hereafter); and (d) going out to dinner, a movie, to gamble, or to hear music or see a play (i.e., going out for enjoyment). Respondents were first asked about each activity's perceived importance (very important, somewhat important, not so important; categorized as very or somewhat important versus not-so-important in this study). Then, they were asked if (a) health and functioning problems and (b) transportation problems ever kept them from doing each activity. We defined health-related barriers as a positive “health and functioning problem” response to any of the four categories of activities (yes = 1, no = 0) and transportation barriers as a positive “transportation problem” response to any of the four categories of activities (yes = 1, no = 0).
2.2.3. Past-month automobile driving status and alternative transportation use
We constructed the driving status variable from the respondent's answers to the questions about driving “to get to places” in the last month, ceasing driving since the last interview (for continuing sample), and the last time when the respondent drove. The three attributes were driving last month (i.e., currently driving), stopping driving last year, stopping driving before last year, or never driving. Respondents were also asked about the use of alternative transportation: (a) walking or using a wheelchair or scooter; (b) getting a ride from a family member, friend, or a paid helper; (c) using a van or shuttle service provided by the place of residence or for seniors or people with disabilities; (d) taking public transportation; and (e) taking a taxi, Uber, or Lyft; and (f) using any other means.
2.2.4. Sociodemographic factors
These included age in 2023 (65–74 [reference category], 75–84, 85+); gender (female vs. male); race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White [reference category], non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, all other); marital status (married/partnered vs. single; descriptive purpose only); living arrangement (living alone vs. living with someone); education (bachelor's degree vs. no degree); family income (<$43,000, $43,000-$83,999 [reference category], $84,000+, and missing); residential type (care community vs. own home); and residential location (non-metropolitan vs. metropolitan area).
2.2.5. Cognitive, physical/functional, and psychosocial health indicators
Cognitive health was measured with a self-reported diagnosis of dementia (yes or no), and physical/functional health was measured with (a) the number of chronic medical conditions (0–8: arthritis, cancer, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, lung disease, osteoporosis); (b) use of any mobility assistive devices (cane, walker, wheelchair, or scooter) outside the home (yes or no); (c) activity-limiting fall worry (yes or no); (d) activity-limiting chronic pain (yes or no); and (e) vision problems (could not see well enough to recognize someone across the street with glasses or contacts; yes or no).
Psychosocial health indicators were the self-report of having no one to talk to (yes vs. no) and depression/anxiety symptoms in the past month. In NHATS, depression/anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) (Kroenke et al., 2009). The PHQ-4 includes the first two items (PHQ-2; had little interest or pleasure in doing things, and felt down, depressed, or hopeless) from the 9-item PHQ-9 for depression (Kroenke et al., 2003) and the first two items (GAD-2; felt nervous, anxious, or on edge, and have been unable to stop or control worrying) from the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (Spitzer et al., 2006). Responses to each PHQ-4 item were based on a 4-point scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day), with the total score ranging from 0 to 12. Unweighted Cronbach's alpha for the PHQ-4 for the study sample was 0.77.
2.3. Analysis
All analyses were conducted with Stata/MP 18's svy function (College Station, TX) to account for NHATS's stratified, multistage sampling design. First, we used χ2 tests, ANOVA, and t-tests to compare the characteristics of homebound older adults with two groups of non-homebound older adults, i.e., those who went out 2–4 days a week and those who went out 5+ days a week. Second, we fitted two generalized linear models (GLMs) for a Poisson distribution with a log link to test H1 (association of homebound state vs. having gone out 2–4 days [Model 1] and 5+ days [Model 2] as the dependent variable [DV] with driving status as the independent variable [IV] in both models). Third, we also fitted two GLMs for a Poisson distribution with a log link to test H2 (association of homebound state vs. having gone out 2–4 days [Model 1] and 5+ days [Model 2] as the DV with transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities as the IV in both models). Fourth, we fitted two GLMs for a Poisson distribution with a log link to test H3 (association of transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities as the DV with alternative transportation use as the IVs), one for all non-drivers and the other for homebound non-drivers only. Covariates included health-related barriers to participating in social/community activities, sociodemographic variables, and cognitive, physical/functional, and psychosocial health. We used GLMs rather than logistic regression models because odds ratios exaggerate the true relative risk to some degree when the event (e.g., driving and transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities in this study) is a common (i.e., >10%) occurrence (Grimes and Schulz, 2008). As a preliminary diagnostic, we used variance inflation factor (VIF), using a cut-off of 2.50 (Allison, 2012), from linear regression models to assess multicollinearity among covariates. VIF diagnostics indicated that multicollinearity was not a concern when marital status (correlated with living arrangement) was excluded from the model. GLM results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CI. Significance was set at p<.05.
3. Results
3.1. Characteristics of homebound older adults compared to non-homebound older adults
Table 1 shows that 5.2% (95% CI = 4.6–5.8) of the study population was homebound, 12.1% (95% CI = 11.2–13.2) went out 2–4 days a week, and 82.7% (95% CI = 81.5–83.8) went out 5+ days a week in the past month. Of the homebound older adults, 22.0% were age 85 and older, 71.3% were female, 11.7% were Black, 24.8% were Hispanic, 39.4% lived alone, 11.6% were residents of care facilities, 11.3% reported having no one to talk to, 61.5% had low income (<$43,000), and 7.4% had a dementia diagnosis. They had 3.1 chronic medical conditions on average. More than one-half reported activity-limiting pain, one-third reported activity-limiting fall worry, and almost 60% reported needing help to go outside. Homebound older adults did not significantly differ from those who went out 2–4 days a week on age, gender, living alone, residence in a care facility, and dementia diagnosis. However, compared to both groups of non-homebound older adults, homebound older adults had higher proportions of racial/ethnic minorities, those with income <$43,000, and those having no one to talk to. Homebound older adults also had more physical/functional, psychosocial, and vision health problems than non-homebound older adults.

Compared to their non-homebound peers, fewer homebound older adults reported social/community activities as important to them. However, those who reported the importance of these activities were not a small proportion (e.g., 31.9% reported attending clubs/meetings as important, and 48.6% reported going out for enjoyment to be important). More homebound older adults reported health-related (54.2%) and transportation (32.1%) barriers to these activities than their non-homebound peers. Corresponding percentages were 34.1% and 18.2% among those who went out 2–4 days a week and 15.0% and 6.6% among those who went out 5+ days a week.
Only 27.0% of homebound older adults reported driving at the time of the interview, compared to 57.2% of those who went out 2–4 days and 89.2% of those who went out 5+ days. Most non-driving older adults reported getting a ride from family/friends/paid help, followed by walking, van/shuttle services, taxi/ride-hailing services, and public transportation. Compared to non-driving, non-homebound older adults, lower proportions of non-driving homebound older adults got a ride, walked, and used taxi/ride-hailing services and public transportation.

4. Discussion
In this study, we examined the associations of homebound state in Medicare beneficiaries age 65 and older with their driving status and self-reported transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities. We also examined the associations of self-reported transportation barriers with alternative transportation use among all non-drivers and homebound non-drivers. The findings show that 5.2% of the Medicare beneficiaries in 2023 were homebound, and only a little more than a quarter of the homebound older adults reported driving. Not surprisingly, more than one-half of homebound older adults reported health-related barriers to social/community activities, and nearly one-third reported transportation barriers.
Multivariable analysis results supported H1 and H2 that homebound state, compared to going out 2–4 days or 5+ days a week, was significantly associated with non-driving and transportation barriers, even controlling for health-related barriers and other variables associated with life-space mobility in later life. These results suggest that some older adults’ homebound state is likely partly due to transportation barriers. As shown in a previous study (Szanton et al., 2016) and in this study, a significant proportion of homebound older adults reported the importance of participation in social/community activities. With appropriate and reliable transportation, it would be easier for them to participate in these activities.
Consistent with the findings of a previous study (Choi and DiNitto, 2016), rides from family/friends/paid helpers were the most used means of transportation among non-driving older adults, followed by walking and the use of van/shuttle services. Walking to places is not only an easy way of getting to places but also an incidental source of physical activity when the built environments (e.g., land use mix, street connectivity, traffic conditions, proximity to destinations, safety, and aesthetics) and weather are favorable to walking (Laverty et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018). However, it is not feasible for many disabled older adults, especially for a long-distance trip and inclement weather. Van/shuttle services available in some senior housing complexes and residential care communities and the paratransit services often provided by local transportation agencies are important alternative transportation means for older adults, providing origin-to-destination convenience. However, these services may not be available in low-income senior housing settings and non-urban areas. Paratransit driver shortages due to poor pay and benefits are also problematic (Gleason and Silverstein, 2023). Moreover, the eligibility criteria for paratransit services and the preparation work (e.g., making the reservations and having a contingency plan when a driver does not arrive on time) can be barriers to using the services among some older adults (Silverstein and Turk, 2016).
Less than 10% of homebound non-drivers, respectively, used taxi/ride-hailing services and public transportation. The small proportion of taxi/ride-hailing services and public transportation users suggests that the affordability of taxi/ride-hailing services and the availability and accessibility of public transportation may have been barriers to some older adults. Previous studies of older adults found that living alone and in an urban area, having a college degree, higher income, having a smartphone, and comfort with technology (for booking a trip using an app) were associated with higher odds of using ride-hailing services (Misra et al., 2022; Mitra et al., 2019). Public transportation is not easy for older adults, especially those who are disabled and use mobility assistive devices. The “mobility work” or the physical, emotional, and spatiotemporal effort that older adults must invest in using public transportation includes walking to and from the bus stop, trip planning, stepping onto/off the bus (especially when using a mobility assistive device), finding a seat, carrying items on the bus, calling a stop, and traveling in winter conditions (Ravensbergen et al., 2021). The mobility work required for using public transportation poses significant barriers for disabled older adults.
Interestingly, multivariable analysis results show that only the use of taxi/ride-hailing services among all non-drivers and the use of van/shuttle services among homebound non-drivers were associated with a higher likelihood of reporting transportation barriers to participating in social/community activities. These findings do not support H3. We speculate that some older adults may have used taxi/ride-hailing services for healthcare and other essential functions but found them too costly for social/community activities. Similarly, van/shuttle services are often for healthcare or other essential trips; thus, their availability for social/community activities is limited. Most importantly, the findings show that living alone, being in non-metropolitan areas, and having a college degree were associated with a higher likelihood of reporting transportation barriers among all non-driving older adults and non-driving homebound older adults. These suggest that older adults without or with low social support and living in a non-metropolitan area with sparse transportation services have transportation challenges. The significance of a college degree may suggest that older adults with higher education are more likely to seek participation in social/community activities outside the home but encounter transportation barriers.
This study also shows that the socioecological theory of mobility provides a good explanatory framework for the factors associated with a homebound state. One or more physical/functional health conditions, psychosocial factors (having no one to talk to and depressive/anxiety symptoms), age, gender, race/ethnicity, and income were significantly associated with a homebound state. The consistently significant association between psychosocial factors and homebound state points to social isolation and depression/anxiety in homebound older adults, although it was not possible to discern whether social isolation and depression/anxiety contributed to homebound state or vice versa in a cross-sectional study. Other things being equal, Hispanic older adults were also more likely to be homebound than their non-Hispanic white peers, suggesting that cultural context and individuals’ experiences, opportunities, and behaviors throughout life may have had an influence intersecting with physical and psychosocial factors. For example, an earlier study found that compared to non-Hispanic white older adults, Hispanic older adults were more engaged in activities with children and relatives (Rodríguez-Galán and Falcón, 2010), suggesting possible differences in the type of social activities that different groups of older adults prioritize. Interestingly, a dementia diagnosis was not a significant factor for limited life-space mobility in older adults in this study. Given that we included self-respondents only, those with a dementia diagnosis still likely had functional cognitive health. More research is needed to examine the association between detailed measures of cognitive health and life-space mobility in older adults. Further research is also needed to examine the role of environmental factors in life-space mobility. We factored in residential (non-metro vs. metro) areas but not the built and social environments that have been found to affect life-space mobility. Research has shown that the built environments, neighborhood physical disorder, and neighborhood social cohesion were significantly associated with social isolation in older adults (Qin et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2023).
To our knowledge, this is the first study that examined transportation barriers to social/community activities among homebound older adults. The study's strength includes a large nationally representative sample, but the study also has some limitations. First, only correlation, not causation, can be derived from cross-sectional survey data. Second, all data were self-reported and may have been subjected to recall and social desirability bias. Third, the analytic models did not include indicators of cultural context, other life-course factors, and the built and social environments.
Despite these limitations, the study's findings have important implications for improving the availability and accessibility of transportation to facilitate older adults' participation in social/community activities. Non-driving older adults with physical/functional limitations should be able to use reliable and affordable transportation appropriate to their preferences and capabilities. Improving transportation for these older adults not just for healthcare-related visits but also for social/community activities needs to be a public health policy priority in a rapidly aging society. Providing increased opportunities for homebound older adults to participate in social/community activities by alleviating transportation barriers will help contribute to reducing their social isolation and depression/anxiety. Social integration of these older adults will, in turn, likely exert positive effects on their cognitive and physical/functional health. Along with continued research on autonomous vehicles (Dicianno et al., 2021; Sivakanthan et al., 2024), other approaches include expansion of and/or public subsidies for the following programs: (a) volunteer driver programs that many local communities are implementing, (b) non-profit or local government partnership with ride-sharing services like Uber or Lyft to provide discounted or subsidized rides (e.g., GoGo Grandparent program; https://www.gogograndparent.com/); (c) older adult-friendly and easily accessible public transportation; and (d) on-demand, customized, and door-to-door micro transit and paratransit services. Aging service organizations, local governments, and transportation service programs must also develop assistive apps for older adults to navigate transit.
