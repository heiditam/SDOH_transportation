Abstract

Research at the confluence of accessibility, equity and health is flourishing. And yet, there is only slow and modest progress in terms of improving the spatial and temporal accuracy of measuring accessibility and equity of accessibility to health care services. This paper critically reviews the latest methodological and empirical research developments and trends in this area through a transport geography lens. More specifically, this paper discusses recent accomplishments in terms of modeling accessibility and provides a systematic and comprehensive literature review of their application in empirical studies of health care delivery. Based on this literature review a research agenda is put forward, identifying knowledge gaps that transport researchers can help to fill. These knowledge gaps pertain to the need for more spatially disaggregated, individualized and temporally-aware accessibility metrics, more sophisticated geocomputational tools to operationalize such metrics and improved measurement of equity considerations in empirical research.
Introduction

Health care access is concerned with the processes surrounding the entry of persons and population groups into the health care delivery system. It is a multi-faceted concept that involves five dimensions including affordability (i.e. costs of health care utilization), acceptability (i.e. health service compliance and satisfaction), availability (i.e. adequacy of health service provision), geographic accessibility (i.e. travel impedance between patients and providers) and accommodation (i.e. appropriateness and suitability of health services) (Penchansky and Thomas, 1981). This review paper concentrates on one of these aspects, namely geographic accessibility, and discusses its measurement and operationalization. The scope of this review is limited to geographic accessibility of primary care, hospital care and emergency medical services as these services are seen as the most important contributors to overall population health and have been the focal point of scholarly attention in the recent past.
The motivation for a comprehensive review on this matter is threefold. First, of the aforementioned five dimensions of health care access, it is particularly geographic accessibility, also termed spatial or physical accessibility or simply accessibility, that has gained traction over the past few years, chiefly owing to the increased sophistication of geographic information systems (GIS) and availability of spatially disaggregated data on both health care supply and demand. There is now a wide recognition of the value of GIS in, among others, mapping the spatial distribution of health care needs and utilization, monitoring and evaluating the socio-spatial repercussions of health policy actions, determining optimal health service locations and disentangling the relationships between disparities in accessibility and health outcomes (Higgs, 2004, McLafferty, 2003).
Second, adequate and equitable accessibility is a concern of growing importance to policy makers, public health reformers and practitioners alike since recent years have brought convincing evidence to the fore showing that spatial barriers between consumer (e.g., patient) and provider (e.g., general practitioner) contribute to lower health care utilization and decreased uptake of preventive services, which may in turn give rise to poorer health outcomes. Dai (2010), for example, found that women are more likely to respond positively to invitations to attend mammography screening in Detroit (Michigan) if they lived closer to existing clinics. Hiscock et al. (2008), for their part, noted an inverse relationship between travel time access and utilization of general practitioners (GPs) and pharmacies in a nation-wide study in New Zealand. Another example is Gage and Calixte (2006) who showed that accessibility is a crucial dimension of the use of maternal health care in Haiti. Much of the empirical evidence to date has accrued from rural, sparsely populated areas where people experience distance barriers resulting from diminished local availability of health care provision. But the ‘health accessibility lens’ has also been applied to deprived (e.g., low-income) individuals and communities who tend to have a poorer health status and fewer mobility resources for accessing health services relative to their high-income counterparts (Bissonnette et al., 2012, Kawakami et al., 2011).
Third, while measuring accessibility and equity in accessibility of health services is of straightforward importance in developing countries where spatial coverage problems are often obvious and acute, it also becomes an extremely apposite exercise in the developed world in light of the radical demographic changes taking place in contemporary Western societies. A steady decline in fertility rate over the past half century in combination with increased longevity is engendering a dramatic shift in the population distribution of many industrialized countries towards older ages (Lutz et al., 2008). These emerging cohort effects pose serious challenges for efficiently allocating the available health resources among the population in an equitable manner, especially in a climate of budgetary pressure (Loo et al., 2012). Hence, a realistic portrayal of health care accessibility is key to supporting policy makers in taking judicious rationing decisions that affect the spatial planning of health resources in order to maintain adequate care for a growing and more demanding yet less mobile older, age cohort.
This review paper highlights various methodological and conceptual issues in the measurement of social and spatial inequalities in accessibility of health care services. This review comes at a time when a growing number of voices are calling for temporally integrated perspectives on accessibility that account for behavioral and mobility aspects (Guagliardo, 2004, Kwan, 2012a, Miller, 2007, Páez et al., 2010b). While recent advancements in this active field of transport geography have pushed forward the research frontier in terms of both methodological and empirical aspects, it is argued that measurements of (equity in) accessibility in empirical health studies still tend to be conceptualized predominantly in static spatial terms, creating misleading health care landscapes and concomitant misinformed policy interventions (Dewulf et al., 2013, Hawthorne and Kwan, 2012). This review calls for more spatially disaggregated, individualized and temporally-aware accessibility metrics, increased accuracy in modeling transportation to and demand for health care services, improved measurement of equity considerations and more sophisticated geocomputational tools to operationalize the suggested improvements.
Several recent reviews on health care accessibility can be found in the scholarly literature. Many of these have laid out the advantages of GIS for health care research and its adoption by health researchers and policy makers, including McLafferty, 2003, Higgs, 2004, Joyce, 2009 and Shaw (2012). Others, among them Yang et al., 2006, Apparicio et al., 2008, Schuurman et al., 2010 and Dewulf et al. (2013), have offered a comparative analysis of accessibility measures in particular health settings. Wang (2012) has specifically looked at distance decay and optimization models in this context. This review is different from these previous studies in that it focuses on the implications of the shortcomings of current accessibility measures for understanding equity in access to health care and also provides a more systematic investigation of empirical work on primary, hospital and emergency care. The closest predecessor of our review is perhaps the work by Guagliardo (2004). Apart from updating Guagliardo’s work by discussing the accomplishments made in the last decade, this paper puts accessibility of health care into a different perspective – that is a transport geography perspective rather than a public health perspective. Furthermore, it expands the scope of Guagliardo’s literature review and suggests new pathways for further exploration.
The remainder of this paper comes in four parts. The paper proceeds with an overview of how accessibility has been assessed in previous studies in the context of health care and identifies various methodological issues. Next, a literature review is conducted on the basis of a systematic literature search. From this literature review, a research agenda is derived which offers various suggestions and avenues for future research for transport geographers. The paper concludes with a synthesizing discussion.
