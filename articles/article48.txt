Abstract
Disparities in access to the organ transplant waitlist are well-documented, but research into modifiable factors has been limited due to a lack of access to organized prewaitlisting data. This study aimed to develop a natural language processing (NLP) algorithm to extract social determinants of health (SDOH) from free-text notes and quantify the association of SDOH with access to the transplant waitlist. We collected 261 802 clinician notes from 11 111 adults referred for kidney or liver transplants between 2016 and 2022 at the Duke University Health System. An SDOH ontology and a rule-based NLP algorithm were created to extract and organize terms. Education, transportation, and age were the most frequent terms identified. Negative sentiment and refer were the most negatively associated features with listing in both kidney and liver transplant patients. Income and employment for the kidney, and judgment and positive sentiment for liver were the most positively associated features with the listing. This study suggests that the integration of NLP tools into the transplant clinical workflow could help improve collection and organization of SDOH and inform center-level efforts at resource allocation, potentially improving access to the transplant waitlist and posttransplant outcomes.

1. Introduction
Disparities in access to the organ transplant waitlist are well described.1,2 Patient demographics including female biological sex, non-White race, low educational level, low income, and public insurance are all associated with lower rates of listing for transplant.3, 4, 5 However, prior research into modifiable causal factors that drive inequities in access to transplants has been limited to small studies with varying generalizability, due in part to lack of access to organized data.4,6, 7, 8, 9 These include factors such as social determinants of health (SDOH), the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, and health-related social needs (HRSN), social and economic needs that individuals experience that affect the ability to maintain their health and well-being.10
Access to an organ transplant requires patients to undergo an extensive evaluation process, and candidacy is ultimately determined by a selection committee that weighs the risks and benefits of various health-related, immunologic, surgical, and psychosocial risk factors. Psychosocial evaluation by a transplant social worker is a standard component of the transplant evaluation and provides detailed information about patients and HRSN. Transplant nurse coordinators and other clinicians may also collect additional information through their long-term relationship with patients before and after the transplant. In addition to informing patient care delivery, information about patients’ SDOH and HRSN can provide valuable insight into barriers to transplant at the center, regional, and national levels.
Despite their value, SDOH and HRSN data are typically documented in clinician notes and the unstructured format of free text makes the data difficult to organize, combine, or integrate with clinical data. Natural language processing (NLP) combines computational linguistics with statistical and machine learning models to process, recognize, and organize text.11 The objective of this study was to develop an NLP algorithm to examine the presence of SDOH in clinician free-text notes and quantify the association between SDOH and access to the transplant waitlist.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Source of data
2.1.1. Structured data
Adults referred for abdominal (liver, kidney, or pancreas) transplant between 2016 and 2022 were identified in the Duke University Health System electronic health record (EHR). Cases that were censored (ie, without committee decision) were excluded from the analysis and 92 patients referred for pancreas transplants were excluded due to their small sample size. Demographic information extracted included age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, insurance, primary language, citizenship, employment status, highest education, and marital status. Patients with missing demographics were assigned the “Unknown” label. Medicare, North Carolina Medicaid, out-of-state Medicaid, and Medicaid pending insurance status were grouped as “Public” and all other insurance statuses were reported as “Private.” Patients were assigned 1 of 4 statuses related to access to transplant: referral, evaluation, waitlist, and transplanted. Patients without a waitlist or transplanted status were considered as “not listed” (Table 1).

2.1.2. Unstructured data
We extracted 3 types of clinician free-text notes: progress notes, telephone encounter notes, and transplant selection committee review notes. Because patients undergoing evaluation for transplant have high rates of health care utilization, we restricted progress notes to specific author types: licensed clinical social worker, psychologist, care manager, coordinator, care coordinator, licensed clinical social worker associate, clinical social worker, Master of Social Work, student social work, licensed psychological associate, and caregiver support specialist. Notes without a “signed” or “addendum” status were excluded. This study was approved by the Duke University Institutional Review Board prior to any data collection (protocol number: Pro00110780).
2.2. SDOH ontology (categories and terms)
We created the baseline SDOH ontology using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) PhenX Toolkit SDOH collections, which provides a comprehensive set of standardized measurement protocols used in health equity research.12 The SDOH ontology was then expanded to include 320 terms identified through a literature review and manual review of 50 medical records chosen at random (Supplementary Table 1). The final ontology included the following terms across 20 categories: access to health services, family income, biological sex, birthplace, housing and address, age, employment status, educational attainment, English proficiency, ethnicity and race, food insecurity, gender identity, health insurance, health literacy, occupation prestige, sexual orientation, wealth, job insecurity, access to health technology, and discrimination.
2.3. NLP algorithm
We used the rule-matching engine in spaCy13 (version 3.1.3), an open-source NLP library, to process the free-text notes in Python (version 3.7.6). A set of token-based rules was created to capture the SDOH terms in our ontology considering their lemmas (ie, the base form of a word) and potential parts of speech. Each patient’s notes were joined into one aggregated note and lower-cased. Repetitive white space and special characters including “-” and “?” were replaced with single white space. Then the notes were tokenized and the SDOH terms were captured and counted per patient. The counts were stratified based on transplant listing status and then normalized to the number of patients in each group. We also grouped the counts according to our SDOH ontology categories to calculate the overall occurrence in the notes of each category.
2.4. Sentiment analysis
We performed sentiment analysis of the notes using 2 different approaches. First, Valence Aware Dictionary and Sentiment Reasoner (VADER)14 which is a lexicon and rule-based sentiment analysis tool was used to calculate a compound sentiment score per patient’s notes. The compound score is a single unidimensional measure of sentiment normalized to be between –1 (most negative) and +1 (most positive). Second, a robustly optimized BERT-pretraining approach (RoBERTa)-base model which is trained on 124 million tweets and fine-tuned for sentiment analysis15, 16, 17 was used to calculate positive, neutral, and negative scores per patient’s notes. These 3 scores are from 0 to 1 and normalized so that the sum of them equals 1. RoBERTa, which is based on bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) architecture has a maximal length of 512 tokens. To process longer texts, all the texts were split into 510 tokens per piece, “<s>” and “</s>” tokens were added to the start and end of each piece of the token with “1” added to their attention mask. The remaining token and attention mask space were padded with “<pad>” and “0,” respectively. The output scores were normalized using the softmax function and then the average score of split tokens was calculated and assigned as the score per each note. Finally, the first, second, and third quartiles of the compound, positive, and negative scores were calculated per patient and incorporated in the later association study. Sentiment scores between listed and not listed groups in each organ were compared by the Mann-Whitney U test.
2.5. Manual chart review and evaluation of the NLP algorithm
We reviewed notes of 88 patients (60 kidney and 28 liver) for the occurrence of access to health services, employment status, family income, health insurance, and wealth SDOH categories to evaluate the performance of our NLP algorithm based on sensitivity, precision, and F1 scores and average performance across all the reviewed SDOH categories. We also used a RoBERTa-large zero-shot text classification model16,18 to capture the presence of the same listed SDOH categories in the patients' notes. This model is based on RoBERTa fine-tuned on the Multi-Genre Natural Language Inference corpus,19 which is a collection of 433 000 sentence pairs annotated with their textual entailment. Notes were preprocessed similar to the sentiment analysis to handle the maximal token length of 512. Our hypothesis prompt was added to the end of each split text as “This medical note contains information about patient's SDOH category” and separated from the note text by “</s>” special token so that each split text was followed by the hypothesis and ended with “</s>” token with a total token length of 512. The SDOH categories were the same categories we reviewed in the chart. The 3 output probabilities of contradiction, neutral, and entailment were normalized by softmax function, and average scores were assigned to each patient. If the probability of contradiction was higher than the sum of neutral and entailment, then we considered our hypothesis rejected, ie, the note text does not have information about the specific SDOH category. Finally, we compared our NLP algorithm with the RoBERTa model.
2.6. Multivariable analysis of medical, demographics, sentiment scores, and SDOH terms
To evaluate the association of structured medical and demographics and extracted SDOH terms with the listing status, we developed separate multivariable regularized logistic regression and XGBoost20 gradient-boosted decision tree classification models for kidney and liver patients listing status. First, we only used structured medical and demographics (see Table 1) as the features of the first set of models. Then, the identified SDOH terms per patient were transformed using the term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) and the TF-IDF vector embedding was added to the structured features and incorporated in the second set of the models. TF-IDF is a widely used statistical method in NLP and information retrieval. It measures how important a term is within a document, which here is the collection of extracted terms per patient, relative to a collection of all documents (ie, the corpus of extracted terms from all patients’ notes). Seventy-five percent of the data, stratified by listing status, were randomly selected as the training set, and used to optimize the hyperparameters of each model such as learning rate, maximum depth, number of trees, subsample, and subsample ratio of columns in each tree by 5-fold cross-validation; the remaining 25% of data were used as the testing set. The log odds of structured features and SDOH terms from the logistic regression models were reported to study their association with patient listing status. A high positive or negative coefficient for a term indicates a strong positive or negative association between TF-IDF value (ie, weighted importance) of that term and the likelihood of being listed, respectively. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP)21 values were used to quantify the importance of structured features and SDOH terms in XGBoost models. To evaluate the performance of the models, we reported the F1 score which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall with equal importance and indicates the predictive ability of the models, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC), and the area under the precision-recall curve (AP or average precision). The 95% confidence interval (CI) of the metrics was calculated by bootstrapping with replacement over the training set and evaluating the model performance on the testing set. Bootstrapped values were used to calculate the Student’s t-test for the statistical significance of the performance metric differences.
3. Results
3.1. SDOH term counts
A total of 261 802 free-text notes including 235 238 telephone encounter, 7213 committee review, and 19 351 progress notes were collected from 11 111 adults referred for kidney or liver transplant at the Duke University Health System from 2016 to 2022. Of these included notes, 87 142 contained a total of 707 135 SDOH terms. This included 68 349 telephone encounter notes, 2513 committee review notes, and 16 280 progress notes. The median number of free-text notes per patient containing an SDOH term was 6.0 (interquartile range [IQR] [3.0, 10.0]) for the kidney and 6.0 (IQR [2.0, 11.0]) for the liver. The 3 most frequent SDOH terms overall were education, transportation, and age (Supplementary Fig. S1). This was similar for patients not listed. Education was the most frequently identified term in telephone encounter notes and progress notes whereas $ (indicating a dollar amount) was among the most frequent terms in committee review notes.
3.1.1. SDOH terms by organ, listing status, and note type
We first examined the frequency of individual SDOH terms by organ, listing status, and note type (Supplementary Fig. S1). Education, transportation, and living situation (hereafter mentioned as live) were the most frequent terms identified in patients referred for kidney transplant, while education, age, and live were the top 3 identified terms in patients referred for liver transplant.
Among patients referred but not listed for a kidney transplant: Medicare, education, and transportation were most frequently mentioned in telephone encounter notes; education, transportation, and live were most frequently observed in progress notes; and refer, $ (indicating a dollar amount), and age were the most frequently observed in committee review notes.
Among patients referred but not listed for liver transplant: refer, Epic MyChart, and value were the most frequently observed in telephone encounter notes, education, age, and live were the most frequently observed in progress notes, and nutrition, preventative, and $ (indicating a dollar amount) were the most frequently observed in committee review notes.
3.1.2. SDOH categories by organ, listing status, and note type
Each term was assigned to the appropriate SDOH category, and the frequency of these categories was examined by organ, listing status, and note type (Supplementary Fig. S2). Access to health, family income, and education status were the most frequent categories identified in patients referred for kidney transplant, versus access to health, education status, and family income in patients referred for liver transplant.
In kidney transplants, ethnicity and race, and discrimination categories were more commonly observed in telephone encounter notes of patients not listed compared to those listed (0.25 vs 0.20, and 0.12 vs 0.01, respectively). Progress notes contained terms related to access to health, address, health insurance, wealth, biological sex, technology access, food insecurity, ethnicity and race, and discrimination for not listed kidney patients compared to those listed. In committee review notes, access to health, age, biological sex, address, and gender identity categories was more commonly observed for not listed kidney patients.
For liver transplant patients who were not listed, technology access, biological sex, and ethnicity and race categories were more commonly observed in their telephone encounter notes compared to listed patients (1.48 vs 1.44, 1.09 vs 1.01, and 0.39 vs 0.32, respectively). Progress notes had higher occurrences of access to health, health insurance, wealth, biological sex, technology access, ethnicity and race, discrimination, and food insecurity categories for not listed patients. In committee review notes, access to health, age, employment status, job insecurity, discrimination, and occupational prestige categories were more frequent for not listed patients.

We measured the performance of XGBoost models with only structured medical and demographic covariates and compared them with XGBoost models with added sentiment scores and TF-IDF values of extracted SDOH terms to the features. The F1 score, AUC, and AP for kidney model with only structured data were 0.62 (95% confidence interval [CI] [0.61-0.64]), 0.78 (95% CI [0.77-0.79]), and 0.52 (95% CI [0.50-0.53]), respectively (Fig. 3A). Adding sentiment scores and TF-IDF vector embedding of identified SDOH terms increased the F1 score, AUC, and AP by 42%, 22%, and 68% to 0.89 (95% CI [0.88-0.89]), 0.95 (95% CI [0.95-0.96]), and 0.87 (95% CI [0.86-0.88]), respectively (Fig. 3B). The liver model with only structured data achieved an F1 score of 0.58 (95% CI [0.55-0.60]), AUC of 0.68 (95% CI [0.66-0.70]), and AP of 0.38 (95% CI [0.34-0.41]) (Fig. 4A). Similarly, adding sentiment scores and TF-IDF values of extracted SDOH terms increased the F1 score, AUC, and AP by 46%, 35%, and 100% to 0.85 (95% CI [0.83-0.86]), 0.92 (95% CI [0.91-0.93]), and 0.75 (95% CI [0.72-0.79]), respectively. All comparisons were statistically significant (P < .001). We also compared the performance of models with structured and unstructured-extracted data for Black vs White and female vs male kidney and liver patients which showed similar performance (Supplementary Table 2).

4. Discussion
Despite longstanding knowledge of disparities in access to transplants, most research remains reliant on associations between patient demographics or community-level SDOH as primary causal factors.1,5,22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Meanwhile, many questions remain related to the influence of individual SDOH and HRSN on access to and outcomes following organ transplant. Secondary analysis of EHR data is a well-established method for improving understanding of clinical course, enabling risk prediction, and identifying targets for interventions.27 A persistent barrier to progress in this area is the availability, organization, and quality of SDOH data, despite the nearly universal practice of formal preoperative social worker evaluation.28 This study reports the results of an NLP algorithm developed to extract and organize SDOH data from EHR free-text notes based on an ontology of 320 SDOH-related terms. Our developed algorithm successfully collected more than 700 000 terms from the free-text notes of more than 11 000 patients referred for a transplant between 2016 and 2022. The terms were successfully organized into 20 SDOH categories based on the PhenX toolkit SDOH collections. Overall, we found differing SDOH term counts by organ and listing status. Adding sentiment scores and TF-IDF values of the identified SDOH terms increased the performance of our predictive models by 22% to 100%. Our results demonstrate that NLP could offer an efficient means to collect SDOH data from free-text notes, which can be challenging to obtain through traditional methods and help elucidate drivers of disparities in access to transplants.29 Automating this process can enable timely review and identification of targets for interventions, which may benefit the identified patients with social support needs.
Our findings mirror previous use of NLP for extracting SDOH from free-text clinical notes in other specialties. Two recent systematic reviews highlight the value of NLP methods to automatically transform clinical text into structured clinical data in patients with chronic diseases in general, and the specific value of NLP for extracting SDOH.30,31 Of note, research related to the use of this technology to advance care in transplantation was not identified in either review. Our focus in this study was to provide an SDOH ontology and create a rule-based NLP algorithm as such rules promote more interpretability and explainability. Our work takes a unique approach to the rule-based approach in that we used the NIH PhenX toolkit SDOH collections as a baseline classification system, augmented by expert clinician input following chart review. The use of the NIH PhenX toolkit SDOH collections is valuable in that it provides standardized definitions for frequent data elements that can facilitate comparisons with other studies and validation in other settings. Other rule-based approaches using keyword matching/counts, regular expressions, or similarity matching have been deployed in a variety of clinical specialties, using methods such as topic modeling32 and deep learning models30 to improve the capabilities of automated extraction and curation of SDOH for clinical use.
There are several known NLP methods of extraction, including rule-based, supervised machine learning, and deep learning approaches. Although no method is universally considered superior, we chose a rule-based method to allow us to build on the PhenX toolkit SDOH collections, incorporate human expertise, and obviate the need for a large training dataset. Two prior studies compared methods of NLP extraction in a specific clinical context. Wu et al33 compared rule-based extraction to deep learning and regularized logistic regression approaches for the identification of SDOH relevant to patients with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. Malmasi et al34 compared rule-based extraction to several alternative approaches including sentence-level support vector machine-based classification and token-level sequence labeling using conditional random fields. Rule-based approaches outperformed alternative methods in both cases. With the rapid rise of transformer-based large language models (LLMs), the performance and generalizability of rule-based NLP algorithms have been studied and compared with LLMs in different contexts. Hua et al35 showed that fine-tuned ClinicalBERT outperformed a rule-based approach to identifying gender identity. Another study demonstrated that fine-tuned Bio_ClinicalBERT and the GatorTron achieved higher performance across 3 different health systems compared to rule-based NLP to identify suicidal behaviors.36 However, Patra et al37 reported that rule-based NLP outperformed fine-tuned FLAN-T5-XL in extracting social support information. Similarly, rule-based NLP demonstrated superior performance than GPT-3.5 turbo in extracting physical rehabilitation exercise information.38 We also showed that our rule-based NLP algorithm outperformed a RoBERTa-large model in precision and F1 scores which is similar to others reporting higher precision in rule-based NLP compared to higher sensitivity in LLMs38,39 which could be due to the accurate but limited number of rules. The pretrained LLMs are usually not trained to detect SDOH, and these differing results demonstrate the need for a more comprehensive comparison of the rule-based NLP algorithms to LLMs.
Employing NLP techniques to extract context-aware SDOH data from free-text clinical notes may have value at multiple levels of influence. For patients with adverse SDOH, identification of HRSN and improved connection with resources for social support may afford them a life-saving chance to access the transplant waitlist. At the transplant center level, NLP and other artificial intelligence–based technologies can be integrated into the transplant clinical workflow to increase the efficiency of the social worker assessment visit and selection committee review. The addition of sentiment analysis increased the performance of our predictive model and may offer an opportunity to identify clinician bias, either implicit or explicit, and prevent undue influence on candidacy assessments. Artificial intelligence–assisted data collection and organization can also be paired with patient screening tools to identify HRSN that are of high priority to patients (eg, transportation services, food banks) and inform center-level efforts at resource allocation. The association between education and citizenship with listing status and their high degree of missingness in a structured manner in the EHR highlights the need for an NLP strategy to accomplish this. Although HRSN are high-priority targets for increasing access to the transplant waitlist, HRSN are highly likely to also influence the waitlist (eg, waitlist removal, inactivation) and posttransplant outcomes (eg, readmissions, rejection, graft survival). Improved identification of HRSN among patients on the transplant waitlist and posttransplant can reveal new insights into the role of HRSN across the entire continuum of transplant care.
Strengths of this study include the rigorous development of the parent ontology using qualitative methods, manual chart review, and the NIH PhenX toolkit SDOH collections. We applied our algorithm to a large cohort of adult patients referred for transplant, allowing a comparison of SDOH between those patients successfully listed for transplant and those who were declined. Our study has limitations worth noting. First, our analysis is limited to a single-center cohort of adults referred for abdominal transplants. Site-specific evaluation is required if it is intended to be used at other health care systems. There is also potential for residual confounding, and EHR SDOH data are often incomplete, making it challenging to assess the influence of SDOH on any target outcome. However, the clinical social work assessment is a standard part of the transplant evaluation and most SDOH with a direct relation to candidacy are recorded; hence, our approach is unlikely to miss important SDOH when both structured and unstructured data are used. Our current ontology may omit other relevant SDOH terms. To ensure a more comprehensive list, future work could involve using neural networks, similarity search techniques, and expert review to identify and include additional terms. Our group has undertaken chart review and annotation of more patient notes to later expand SDOH ontology and qualitatively validate our current and future NLP tools. Finally, rule-based extraction of SDOH terms does not consider the context around the identified terms in the text. For example, texts such as “… refer to certified social worker for follow up” or “… needs to secure transportation” may indicate an SDOH, but “… patient has been having some difficulty with word finding, refer to brain MRI” may not be related to SDOH. The presence of both females and males with similar influence patterns on liver listing status and other SDOH terms such as refer with different context-dependent meanings highlights the potential value of context-aware extraction of SDOH terms. This approach along with annotated chart notes could pave the way for the next iteration and evaluation of our NLP model by fine-tuning LLMs for improved accuracy.
5. Conclusion
These results demonstrate the feasibility and utility of extracting SDOH data from unstructured clinician notes and organizing it for the large-scale study of health disparities. Incorporating these enhanced SDOH into studies of disparities in access to transplants can facilitate investigations of complex causal mechanisms between SDOH and health service delivery and outcomes.
