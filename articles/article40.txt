Abstract
Built environmental determinants can drive lifestyle behaviors and potentially reduce chronic disease prevalence. Few studies exist that have examined the association of obesogenic environment exposures with cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes. We aim to specifically examine the association between neighborhood walkability, food environment and CVD. Data from 6117 Dutch participants of The Maastricht Study, between the ages of 40 and 75 years in the Netherlands were examined. Home addresses were linked to geographic information systems data from the Geoscience and Health Cohort Consortium to create neighborhood exposures of walkability and food environment. Perceived walkability was obtained from the Abbreviated Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale. An 11-year incidence of CVD was defined by self-reported non-fatal or fatal event (as registered by Statistics Netherlands). Cox regression models examined the association of environment exposures with incident CVD adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic variables. There was lower incidence of CVD using the perceived walkability questionnaire in those living in the most walkable neighborhood (Quartile 4 HR: .77; 95% CI = .62, .97) but not using the objective walkability index (Quartile 4 HR: 1.10; 95% CI = .89, 1.38). There was no association between the food environment and incident CVD (Quartile 4 HR: .82; 95% CI = .65, 1.04). The discordant findings between walkability measures suggest integrating residential feedback and accounting for lived experiences should be prioritized by policymakers when designing equitable neighborhoods to prevent CVD.

1. Background
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is responsible for almost half the deaths in Europe and represents 32% of deaths globally (Nichols et al., 2014; Timmis et al., 2022). CVD is also one of the most preventable, non-communicable conditions driven by lifestyle habits which can be reduced through a higher quality diet in fruits and vegetables and regular physical activity, among other behaviors (Chiuve et al., 2008; Åkesson et al., 2007). Research in past decades has shown associations between certain neighborhoods characteristics that may influence its residents towards these favorable habits (Bhatnagar, 2017; Caspi et al., 2012). Some of these attributes consist of food environments with fewer unhealthy offerings within local access and walkable neighborhoods to promote active transport to destinations in addition to leisure time physical activity.
In recent years, higher walkable neighborhood environments (consisting of green space, mixed retail and housing, high sidewalk density, etc) have shown a generally positive relationship with physical activity time (Cerin et al., 2017). The initial use of perceived instruments early on and now objective municipal data to measure walkability has complicated this interpretation (Peters et al., 2020; McCormack et al., 2008). Perceived scales are able to gather the level of satisfaction individuals feel about access to physical activity resources and if the environment attributes are relevant to the needs of those in the community (Peters et al., 2020). Likewise, individuals may inadvertently blame their environment despite having adequate access to recreational resources or are unaware of these facilities nearby. Previous work has shown that residents may overestimate or underestimate distances to common destinations (McCormack et al., 2008). An objective measure may precisely quantify walking path distances and recreation areas but may miss sidewalks that are unkept or a park that is often closed (Weden et al., 2008). The utility for using both types of scales is warranted to account for these discrepancies.
The current literature has predominately examined behaviors and ensuing disease risk factors associated with walkability (Chandrabose et al., 2022). A meta-analysis by Chandrabose and colleagues found an association between higher walkability and lower rates of obesity along with hypertension (Chandrabose et al., 2019). A review by Lai et al. discovered a consistent protective effect of higher walkability on hypertension and arterial stiffness (Lai et al., 2023). However, findings from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis discovered participants in the most walkable areas had higher triglycerides and blood pressure over time (Braun et al., 2016).
Work on disease outcomes has been limited and generally inconclusive in investigating the influence of walkability on CVD (Chandrabose et al., 2022). Tamosuinas and colleagues evaluated green space and CVD outcomes in Lithuania where they found no relationship between distance from green spaces and CVD (Tamosiunas et al., 2014). Using variables of urban compactness comprising of residential density, mixed land use, street connectivity, and centeredness, a US study found an inverse relationship between urban sprawl and coronary heart disease events (Griffin et al., 2013). Collected across several US urban and rural communities, a study by Garg et al. found a reduced risk of CVD events within a 5-km radius from one's home with a high density of physical activity facilities (Garg et al., 2021). Finally, a recent review by Chandrabose and colleagues found a strong association between higher neighborhood walkability and lower obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes (T2DM), but no conclusive evidence with CVD (Chandrabose et al., 2022).
Findings regarding the relationship between food environment and diet quality of those in the surrounding areas have arrived to a less definitive conclusion than theorized (Caspi et al., 2012; Cobb et al., 2015; Gustafson et al., 2012). These studies have been complicated by an array of environment definitions that are often limited to a specific type of food outlet (ex. fast-food, supermarkets) as well as varying neighborhood measures (Caspi et al., 2012; Gustafson et al., 2012). There continues to be no consensus on the utility of postal codes, census tracts, or buffers sizes when defining how one is exposed within the food environment (Caspi et al., 2012). Additionally, diet has been predominately defined by fruit and vegetable intake while omitting other aspects such as sugar-sweetened beverages consumption, processed meat servings, and other deleterious foods that can contribute to premature onset of disease states (Caspi et al., 2012). Despite inconsistent results with diet outcomes, using risk factors as an outcome has shown that areas with dense fast-food availability have been linked with higher BMI, elevated blood pressure, and metabolic risk (Malambo et al., 2016). In some studies, proximity to supermarkets and grocery stores have been shown to be associated with lower weight status while convenience stores with higher weight (Leal and Chaix, 2011).
Literature regarding the relationship between the food environment and CVD is less studied. A systematic review of 15 reviews by Meijer et al. discovered neighborhoods with higher concentrations of fast-food restaurants associated with higher rates of CVD (Meijer et al., 2023). There was not enough data to make a definitive statement regarding other food sources such as restaurants and grocery stores. Individuals are exposed to a multitude of food outlet types and conclusions made from one type of food source would be incomplete (Milanlouei et al., 2020).
Due to a lack of longitudinal studies, the body of research involving the relationship between built environment characteristics and CVD outcomes has not yet reached a consensus (Chandrabose et al., 2022; Drewnowski et al., 2020). Additional assessments using both neighborhood walkability and food environment are warranted to understand how these exposures may affect CVD outcomes. The aim of this study is to investigate the association of neighborhood walkability and food environment with incident CVD in a population-based setting. We hypothesize that those living in more walkable neighborhoods and healthier food environments will have a lower incidence of CVD.
2. Methods
2.1. Population
Data were used from The Maastricht Study, an observational prospective population-based cohort study. The rationale and methodology have been described previously (Schram et al., 2014). In brief, the study focuses on the etiology, pathophysiology, complications, and comorbidities of T2DM and is characterized by an extensive phenotyping approach.
Eligibility for participation were individuals between the ages of 40 and 75 years and living in the southern part of the Netherlands. Participants with and without diabetes were recruited through mass media campaigns, from the municipal registries, and the regional Diabetes Patient Registry via mailings. Recruitment was stratified according to known T2DM status, with an oversampling of individuals with T2DM for reasons of efficiency. The present report includes data from the first 9188 participants, who completed the baseline survey between November 2010 and October 2020. The examinations of each participant were performed within a time window of 3 months. Data from 6117 participants from the original 9188 were eligible for analysis (Fig. 1). Missing data on education, used to measure socioeconomic status, was discovered in 128 individuals. There were 1482 participants excluded due to a history of CVD and 1039 were missing data on walkability and/or food environmental exposures. There were 422 participants data from the Statistics Netherlands mortality database could not be obtained.

2.2. Neighborhood walkability and food environment exposures
Data from the Maastricht Study were enriched with geographic information systems (GIS) data from the Geoscience and Health Cohort Consortium (GECCO). These linkages were done using postal codes/addresses by the data managers following the methods we have previously described (Lakerveld et al., 2020).
We used an objectively measured neighborhood walkability index which was calculated based on a combination of selected components derived and validated that has been described elsewhere (Lam et al., 2022). In short, the following components were included in the index: 1) population density: The number of inhabitants per square kilometer, 2) density of retail and service destinations: The proportion of area retail, hospitality, social services and hospitality, 3) land-use mix: Area devoted to residential, commercial, social cultural, public service and office space, and greenspace, 4) street connectivity: Density of ≥ 3-way intersections, 5) green space: Area devoted to parks, forests, and cemeteries, 6) sidewalk density: Proportion of sidewalk area, 7) and public transportation density: Public transportation stops accessible on foot or by bike. The z-scores of the components were added and divided by the number of components and then re-scaled from 0 to 100 with higher scores representing higher walkability levels.
We chose the walkability data from 2012, 2015 and 2019 as these were closest in time to our enrollment periods (representative of the participants current neighborhood) and contained data from all seven environmental characteristics. Walkability scores of the components retrieved from year 2012 were used for participants entering the Maastricht Study between 2010 and 2013. Walkability scores from 2015 were used for participants enrolled in the Maastricht Study between 2014 and 2016. The remaining participants entering the study until 2019 were assigned to the 2019 enrollment group. The 1000m Euclidian buffer zone around each home address for each participant was chosen as the default distance for the main analysis. To facilitate comparisons and allow for possible non-linear associations, walkability was further ranked into quartiles with the first quartile (Q1) representing the least walkable neighborhoods and fourth (Q4) the highest.
Perceived walkability was obtained from the Abbreviated Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale (ANEWS) that was gathered at baseline for the Maastricht Study. This validated instrument included statements about the neighborhood in relation to walkability across eight domains (Cerin et al., 2006). The dimensions included were classified into land-use mix (Proximity to retail and public destinations), infrastructure (Sidewalk maintenance and cycling safety), and traffic (Safety from motor vehicles). The responses were adapted into a 5-point scale in the Dutch language as follows: 1) Strongly agree, 2) Agree, 3) Neutral, 4) Disagree, 5) Strongly Disagree. Similar to the walkability index, the walkability score was also divided into quartiles.
The food environment was created using data from LOCATUS, a commercial company that audits all food retailers in the Netherlands on 2–3 year cycles (Canalia et al., 2020). A Delphi study conducted by an expert panel was performed to create an aggregate measure of the quality of the retail categories in terms of the healthiness of the products sold, generating the Food Environment Healthiness Index (FEHI)(Poelman, 2018). The FEHI index classifies each type of food retailer in relation to the nutritional quality of the foods they offer on a scale from −5.0 for the least healthy to +5.0 for the healthiest (Refer to Supplementary Table 1 for a comprehensive list of food retailer rating). Positive scores were considered healthy and negative scores were unhealthy. Kernal density decay was used to integrate food retail density, distance, and healthiness. A Euclidian buffer of 1000m from a participant's home address was used to create individualized food environments (Fig. 2). Further details on this scale have been described in previous studies (Timmermans et al., 2018; Chan et al., 2023a).

2.3. Cardiovascular disease
History of CVD was obtained at baseline and derived from the Rose questionnaire and included self-reported history of the following conditions: myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular infarction or hemorrhage, percutaneous artery angioplasty, or vascular surgery on the coronary, abdominal, peripheral, or carotid arteries (ROSE, 1962).
New cases of CVD during follow-up were assessed annually by an online follow-up questionnaire over up to 7.2 years median follow-up until December 2020. Questions were asked if a participant had developed any new disease/condition in the past 12 months by the question, “in the past 12 months, has a doctor told you had the following disease condition … [yes/no/I don't know]”. From this, non-fatal CVD events were defined as the first occurrence of myocardial infarction, cardiovascular disease, and/or peripheral vascular disease. Fatal events were retrieved from Statistics Netherlands (CBS) and defined as ICD-10 Codes I00-I99 and G45-G46.
Survival time for CVD cases was computed as the midpoint between the follow-up date at which the event was reported or the fatal event occurred (whichever occurred first) and the previous follow-up date. Survival time for censored cases was computed as the time of the last available follow-up date. For participants with an incident CVD event who had missing values in the previous follow-up point, we subtracted 182.6 days (half a year) from the report date. We then computed the days between baseline measurement and survival time and divided by 365.25 to compute survival time in years.
2.4. Covariates
Covariates in the main analysis were age, sex, T2DM (because of oversampling), and education (attained). Education was divided into low (no education, primary education, and lower vocational education); intermediate (general secondary education, general vocational education, and higher secondary and pre-university education); or high (higher vocational education and university). The presence of T2DM was measured by a glucose tolerance test and medication use (Schram et al., 2014). An additional covariate used in the sensitivity analyses was housing value. Neighborhood housing value was obtained from Statistics Netherlands and provided by GECCO (Lakerveld et al., 2020). Participant housing value was calculated using the mean postcode housing value in Euros across the data collection period from 2010 to 2017.
2.5. Statistical analysis
Baseline characteristics were summarized using means and standard deviations (continuous variables), counts, and percentages (categorical variables). Proportional hazards assumptions were met and Kaplan-Meier curves were examined. A Cox regression analysis was performed with CVD incident time-to-event outcomes. The Maastricht Study and Statistics Netherlands data were matched to identify incidence, which was a participant without a history of CVD. The exposures were perceived walkability, the objective walkability index, and food environment in separate models adjusted for age, sex, education level, and T2DM status (to account for oversampling). Sensitivity analyses performed included analyzing expanded buffer sizes of the next available distance increment for the objective walkability index (1650m) and food environment (1500m); substitution of education for housing value; effect modification to test for significant interactions with sociodemographic variables including age, sex, T2DM, and education.

Sensitivity analyses revealed no statistical significance with the models using expanded buffer sizes of 1500m for the food environment (Q2 HR: 1.03; 95% CI = .82, 1.29; Q3 HR: .93; 95% CI = .76, 1.14; Q4 HR: .86; 95% CI = .67, 1.11) and 1650m using the objective walkability index (Q2 HR: 1.03; 95% CI = .82, 1.29; Q3 HR: 1.03; 95% CI = .82, 1.30; Q4 HR: 1.05; 95% CI = .83, 1.32) as shown in Supplementary Table 3. The model adjusting for housing value in place of education was similar to the main analyses with lower incidence of CVD in the highest walkable quartile using the perceived questionnaire; and no significant association with the objective walkability index and food environment (Untabulated). Interaction analyses discovered, particularly for women and for the low education group, a lower CVD incidence when living in more perceived walkable neighborhoods (Supplementary Table 4); the lowest education group also benefitted the most from living in a healthy food environment (Supplementary Table 5).
4. Discussion
We aimed to evaluate the relationship between neighborhood walkability and food environment with incident CVD. It was discovered that higher self-perceived walkability was associated with lower CVD risk. We, however, did not find a relationship between the objective neighborhood walkability index and incident CVD. Additionally, the food environment was not associated with CVD incidence.
Participants in our study were found to have a lower incidence of CVD according to the perceived walkability questionnaire. Li and colleagues found a similar association of higher walkability using the NEWS scale and lower CVD incidence in a Chinese sample (Li et al., 2024). The authors discovered land-mix use and crime were the most significant subcomponents. In our study, the objective walkability index showed higher CVD incidence though not significant. Earlier work on this sample yielded discordant findings discovering higher accelerometer measured physical activity using the perceived scale but lower levels using the objective walkability index (Chan et al., 2023b). Although both scales address walkability, each is comprised of different components. The perceived questionnaire solicits opinions on aesthetics, safety, traffic, and ability to navigate their neighborhood amongst other elements. This presentation contrasts from the walkability index scale which is a direct measure of land mix-use and density of several components that provide more opportunities for active transportation. There is no specific radius that the perceived questionnaire is confined to while the objective walkability index has a defined area. Even with the expanded buffer in our sensitivity analyses, the objective walkability index findings remained similar as the main analyses. A purely quantitative index may be limited to the isotropic assumption that individuals equally utilize all parts of their neighborhood environment (Duncan and Kawachi, 2018). Residents may just need a main street artery to access common amenities while the rest of their neighborhood may appear deficient. As noted earlier, the perceived measure may be more representative of how participants engage with their neighborhood as there are certain streets with destinations that they may rarely utilize even though they are near to their home. Integrating residential feedback and accounting for lived experiences should be prioritized by policymakers when designing equitable neighborhoods.
Our findings did not show an association between the food environment and incident CVD. These results are consistent with previous work with this cohort which did not find an association between food environment and diet quality (Chan et al., 2023a). In a systematic review, Meijer and colleagues found a relationship between higher fast-food density and higher incidence of CVD (Meijer et al., 2023). Of the 15 studies reviewed, only 3 included exposures outside of fast food. Daniel et al. evaluated fast food in addition to fruit and vegetable stores and found no relationship between availability of these retailers and CVD in a Canadian sample (Daniel et al., 2010). A study by Mooney et al. found both healthy and unhealthy food sources were associated with higher odds of cardiac arrest (Mooney et al., 2018). Lovasi and colleagues did not find an association between healthy food retailers and CVD mortality in a sample of 2.7 million Americans (Lovasi et al., 2021). Similar to these works, we analyzed quality of food establishments within a defined radius from our participants but were unable to infer utilization. Individuals may purchase unhealthy foods in a market that is categorized as healthy (Neckerman, 2014). Additionally, it is not uncommon to shop for groceries beyond one's neighborhood (Cannuscio et al., 2013; Sohi et al., 2014). To reduce mismatch between neighborhood and the actual activity space of our participants, further research should consider implementing location-based tracking to examine sources of food purchases while maintaining individual privacy (Tamura et al., 2018). However, even when using GPS-data to model actual exposure, early work has shown associations remain inconsistent (Siddiqui et al., 2024).
Subgroup analyses found a protective effect with women but not men in more walkable neighborhoods using the perceived scale. Due to traditional gender roles, it may be that women have more exposure at home and the surrounding neighborhood as men have more labor participation and weekly hours at work compared to women in the Netherlands (Statistics Netherlands). Chum and colleagues discovered more time at home modified the relationship between the food environment and vegetable and fruit intake (Chum and O'Campo, 2015). Further studies on specific populations who have more neighborhood exposure such as those who work from home and retirees are warranted in determining the effects of the built environment and health behaviors. Additionally, lower SES groups appear to have lower incident CVD in the highest perceived walkable neighborhoods and food environments. It may be that higher income individuals will engage in physical activity and decide on food purchases regardless of what their neighborhood has to offer. Built environments may attenuate inequalities experienced by low SES groups by providing less constrained access to health promoting resources.
We analyzed two of the most common built environment determinants that are associated with addressing physical activity and diet quality which have an immense impact on CVD prevention. Other environmental determinants may concurrently interact, confound, or possibly neutralize what may be considered ideal in lowering CVD. For example, a walkable neighborhood with healthy food retailers may be adjacent to environmental noise and air pollution from a roadway. Chronic exposure to excessive noise and air pollutants has been shown to elevate blood pressure and increase stress which can contribute to CVD (Koohsari et al., 2020; Münzel et al., 2014). The dynamic interactions between that which an individual encounters and ensuing events, behavior and physiological consequences are quite complex. Finally, the social environment within a neighborhood may influence utilization of walking infrastructure and food environment (Chan et al., 2024). Participation in social activities and recommendations from neighbors may direct an individual's frequency of attendance to food retailers regardless of their health rating. Future work should consider inclusion of additional simultaneous exposures, built and social environment, in identifying combinations that may prevent the development of CVD.
4.1. Limitations
There were several limitations with our study. The median follow-up duration was 7.2 years while the mean age of our sample was 59.8. Future research would likely benefit from longer follow-up periods to increase power, especially with mortality cases. Second, though we used a validated walkability scale, these newly developed measures are not without imperfections. Future efforts to refine our objective walkability measure such as re-scaling and re-weighting components can improve upon the validity of this measure. Additionally, across a decade, there is a certain degree of food establishment turnover as well as construction that may alter transportation infrastructure. Participants were matched to the latest measure of the walkability index and food environment measures closest to their entrance into the study. Incorporating follow-up measures on the environment including additional administrations of the perceived walkability questionnaire, as well as collecting historical exposure, would account for temporal changes in both walkability and food environment that might impact CVD.
4.2. Strengths
Our study has several strengths. First, this is one of a few studies that examined the relationship between the built environment, specifically walkability and food environment, and CVD incidence. Second, we incorporated multiple aspects of the built environment as opposed to examining a single variable for our objective walkability index which is more representative of what individuals are exposed to in their neighborhoods. Previous studies have focused on a single measure such as green space or walking paths while our objective walkability index and perceived questionnaire encompassed a variety of dimensions that may encourage or dissuade physical activity. Also, the use of the perceived measure served as an informal proxy for usability compared to the objective walkability index which was a measure of availability. Third, we used a direct measure of the food environment including a comprehensive audit of all food retailers with varying degrees of healthiness and thus, a more complete representation of what our participants encounter within their neighborhoods. We feel utilizing a rating system was more informative than relying on area food retailer counts. Finally, we evaluated two exposures of the built environment as individuals encounter multiple elements of the environment at once. Few studies have investigated multiple exposures of the built environment simultaneously.
5. Conclusion
In our sample, we found an association between higher perceived walkability and lower CVD risk. However, there was no association with the objective walkability index and food environment. Perceptions of neighborhood walkability, rather than direct measures of built environment characteristics, appear to identify neighborhood characteristics that have an impact on CVD. Soliciting feedback from residents should be a priority for policymakers in developing equitable neighborhoods that offer amenities that can prevent CVD.
