Abstract
Introduction
Social protection programmes such as the Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) programme are meant to improve access to services including formal healthcare and enhance quality of life of beneficiaries. However, beneficiaries are still faced with various barriers to services such as formal healthcare.
Objective
In this study, with the aid of socio-economic and demographic factors, we predict the likelihood of transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older people under the LEAP programme in Ghana.
Results
The study finds that females are significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation compared with their male counterparts (AOR = 2.22; C1 = 0.869–5.673; P = 0.006). Also, we find that respondents aged 85 years or above are significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation than the other age groups (AOR = 1.66; C1 = 0.860–3.710; P = 0.003). Furthermore, we reveal that participants who receive a monthly income of GHS 201 or more are significantly less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation (AOR = 0.784; CI = 0.313–1.962; P = 0.003).
Implications and conclusion
The results suggest that gender-based development programmes (education and skills training) at the early stages of life would be needed to ensure economic empowerment, adequate preparation towards old age, and reduce transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older people especially the females. Based on the findings, we conclude that a genuine commitment on the part of social programme implementers to improve cash transfer amounts and ensure regular transfers is likely to lessen transportation barriers among the poor older people in formal healthcare utilisation.

1. Introduction
Healthcare utilisation is influenced by the rate at which healthcare users can interact with space and take advantage of the spatial opportunities (healthcare) distributed across space (McCausland et al., 2019; Sherman et al., 2005). However, interaction with spatial opportunities, in this case, healthcare, is as a result of the ability of the users to traverse between their dwelling places and the location where healthcare could be accessed. Thus, access to transportation services is a very important factor in the utilisation of healthcare, especially if healthcare users are to travel long distances to access health services (Spees et al., 2019; Arcury et al., 2005; Kelly et al., 2016).
The impacts of transportation constraints are likely to be worse if there are financial barriers that prevent healthcare users from getting paid transportation. According to Akeju et al. (2016), transportation cost is always considered as an additional cost to healthcare utilisation. Rachele, Sugiyama, Turrell, Healy, and Sallis (2018) for instance, studied transportation costs and healthcare utilisation under different household income scenarios and argue that the provision of policies to reduce automobile dependence and facilitating affordable transportation can address health inequality.
In the case of poor older people surviving under the Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) programme in Ghana, transportation constraints are very likely to be immense due to several reasons. The LEAP programme involves cash transfer to orphans or vulnerable children (OVC), poor older people, and persons with an extreme disability who are unable to work. Nevertheless, the LEAP programme has not substantially reshaped households because the transfer amount is low (Amuzu et al., 2010; de Groot et al., 2015; Davis et al., 2014). Additionally, the low cash transfers are not consistently paid to beneficiaries and hence the LEAP programme does not support permanent and regular consumption patterns of goods and services and also manage financial risks (Davis et al., 2014; Foli, 2016). The low amount of transfer money is likely to impact the ability of the poor older people to obtain basic human needs and even pay for transportation services to and from formal healthcare facilities.
In Ghana, the transportation constraints and their impacts on healthcare utilisation are likely to be compounded by the poor road network (Agbenyo et al., 2017; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019a; Peprah et al., 2019; Djan et al., 2019). Generally, transportation difficulties are in the form of barriers to accessing referral health facilities and the delivery of healthcare supplies (Atuoye et al., 2015). However, the factors associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation are likely to be socio-demographic-specific. Therefore, in this study, we examine demographic and socio-economic factors associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older people who are enrolled in the LEAP programme using empirical information from the Atwima Nwabiagya District of Ghana.
Previous studies have mostly focused on the financial dissatisfaction and inadequacies among beneficiaries of the LEAP programme (see Amuzu et al., 2010; Davis et al., 2014; Foli, 2016), and this has been the focus of many studies related to livelihoods and social protection programmes (Muir, 2017; Wulandari and Kassim, 2016; Aker et al., 2016; Hulme and Shepherd, 2003). None of these studies presents models that predict the likelihood of experiencing transportation barriers to healthcare utilisation under social protection programmes such as the LEAP. We fill this gap by developing and presenting models that predict the likelihood of transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation using socio-economic and demographic factors. We used cluster and simple random sampling techniques to recruit the study participants. We further employed interviewer-administered questionnaires as a data collection instrument and present binary logistic regression analyses (bi-variate and multivariate) of the relationship between transportation barriers, socio-economic and demographic factors. While social protection programmes such as the LEAP can alleviate the level of poverty among the poor older people, a thorough review of the programme is likely to reveal the need for further improvements and thus, improve the quality of life of the beneficiaries of the programme. This is the reason why there is a need for a study that investigates the transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older adults under the LEAP programme. In a broader context, our study contributes to the literature on the socio-economic gaps in the quality of life of people under national social intervention programmes.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Data collection procedure
We collected data for this study between June 1 and June 20, 2018. The dataset forms part of a study on ageing, health, lifestyle, and health services carried out in the Atwima Nwabiagya District, which has currently been divided into the Atwima Nwabiagya Municipality and Atwima Nwabiagya North District of Ghana. However, in this study, we used Atwima Nwabiagya District to represent both Atwima Nwabiagya Municipality and Atwima Nwabiagya North District. A cross-sectional survey design was used to collect the data from poor older people who get their livelihoods from the LEAP programme. Based on the criteria used in selecting who poor older people are under the LEAP programme, we defined a poor older person as an individual who is 65 years or above and benefits from the programme. People who meet these criteria were selected as respondents to take part in the survey. These criteria have been defined and used in previous studies (see Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019b; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019c; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019d; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019e; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2020a; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2020b; Oduro Appiah et al., 2020).
We used two sampling techniques in selecting the study participants, and these are the cluster and simple random sampling. In the sampling process, we selected 200 participants residing in 16 communities in the Atwima Nwabiagya District. A power calculation approach was used in arriving at the sample size of 200 (α = 0.05). The details of the approach used in determining the sample size and selecting the study communities have been reported in Agyemang-Duah et al. (2019c), Agyemang-Duah et al. (2019a), Agyemang-Duah et al. (2020a). Among the 200 respondents selected for ageing, health, lifestyle, and health services survey, 170 utilise formal healthcare services (Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019e; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2020a). These 170 poor older people form the sample size of respondents selected for the study.
Data were collected from respondents using interviewer-administered questionnaires. The questionnaires were designed in the English Language. However, they were read in Asante Twi for cultural reasons and for the fact that almost all residents in the Atwima Nwabiagya District speak and understand the language. A comprehensive explanation of the translation procedure and the validity of the research instrument has been reported in Agyemang-Duah et al. (2019c). Data collection was conducted with the aid of three research assistants from the Department of Geography and Rural Development, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), Kumasi, Ghana. Research assistants were trained for 2 days to ensure quality control and compliance with data collection and research ethics. On the field, we closely monitored and supervised the data collection exercise to ensure adherence to quality control procedures and data collection ethics. Every questionnaire administered lasted between 30 and 40 min. Before every questionnaire administration began, we sought oral and written consent from the respondents. Ethics approval was sought and acquired from the Committee on Human Research Publication and Ethics (CHRPE), KNUST School of Medical Sciences and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital, Kumasi, Ghana (reference: CHRPE/AP/311/18).
2.2. Measurements
2.2.1. Outcome variable
We have defined a transportation barrier to healthcare as when a poor older person was supposed to meet healthcare providers or when a poor older person was scheduled to meet healthcare providers but did not do so or delay because of a lack of means of transportation. We measured transportation barriers to healthcare utilisation as a dichotomous variable indicating “no transportation barriers to healthcare utilisation = 0” or “transportation barrier to healthcare utilisation = 1” in the past one year preceding the time of data collection for this study.
2.2.2. Independent variables
The independent or explanatory variables used in this study are gender (1 = male, 2 = female), age (years) (1 = 65–74 years, 2 = 75–84, 3 = 85 or above), ethnicity (1 = Akan, 2 = Non-Akan), religion (1 = Christianity, 2 = Non-Christians), marital status (1 = Single, 2 = Married), education (1 = no formal education, 2 = basic school education, 3 = high school education), income (GH') (1 = 200 or below, 2 = 201–300, 3 = Above 300) and family support (1 = Yes, 0 = No). Whereas gender, ethnicity, religion, marital status and family support were measured as dichotomous variables, education was measured as a nominal variable. Also, we measured age and income as ranked variables.
2.2.3. Analytical framework
In finding the association between the dependent and independent variables, we used descriptive and inferential statistics to analyse the data. We used descriptive statistics, specifically, percentages and frequencies to describe the background characteristics of the study participants. Similarly, the causes of transportation barriers to formal healthcare use among the participants were determined using descriptive statistics. Inferential statistics, specifically, binary logistic regression in the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 20) software was used to establish association between the dependent and predictor variables. The main purpose of using binary logistic regression to model how demographic and socio-economic factors influence transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation was due to the nature of our dependent variable. In using the binary logistic regression, both bivariate and multivariate analyses were done to determine the factors associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare use among poor older people. The modelling process is based on Gujarati and Porter's (2003) framework for model development. In this study, we developed and specified 9 different models written as follows:

3.2. Prevalence of transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation
Approximately 63% of the respondents reported that they encountered transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The factors contributing to transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation include long distance to formal healthcare facilities (16.8%), poor nature of roads (47.7%), high transport cost or charges (29.9%) and the limited number of commercial vehicles plying rural roads (5.6%) (see Table 2).

3.3. Factors explaining transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation
In a bivariate analysis of factors explaining transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation, we find that females are 1.735 times significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation compared to their male counterparts (COR: 1.735; C1: 0.750–4.016, p = 0.002). The study reveals that respondents aged 85 years or above are 1.779 times significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation (COR: 1.779; C1: 0.828–3.824, p = 0.001). The results further indicate that having a high school education significantly decreases the odds of experiencing transportation barriers to formal healthcare use by 0.725 times (COR: 0.725; CI: 0.633–2.702, p = 0.007). The study reveals that participants who receive a monthly income of GH₵201 or more are 0.702 times significantly less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation compared to their counterparts who receive less than GH₵100 (COR: 0.702; CI:0.128–1.343, p = 0.007).
In a multivariate analysis, females are 2.22 times significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation compared to their male counterparts (AOR: 2.22; C1: 0.869–5.673, p = 0.006). The study reveals that respondents aged 85 years or above are 1.66 times significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation (AOR: 1.66; C1: 0.860–3.710, p = 0.003). The results further indicate that having a high school education significantly reduces the likelihood of facing transportation barriers to formal healthcare use by 0.255 times (AOR: 0.255; CI: 0.068–0.958, p = 0.043). The study reveals that participants who receive a monthly income of GH₵201 or more are 0.784 times significantly less likely to face transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation compared to their counterparts who receive less than GH₵100 (AOR: 0.784; CI:0.313–1.962, p = 0.003) (see Table 3).

4. Main findings
The study reveals that 63% of the participants encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The results reveal that long distance to formal healthcare facilities, poor nature of roads, high transport costs or charges and the limited number of commercial vehicles plying rural roads are the factors contributing to barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The findings indicate that gender, age, educational level and monthly income are associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. We find that females are significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. We find that respondents aged 85 years or above are significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The results indicate that participants with a high school education are significantly less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The findings further show that participants who receive a monthly income of GH₵201 or more are significantly less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation.
5. Discussion
In this study, we examine the demographic and socio-economic characteristics associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older people who benefit from the LEAP programme. In this study, we find that 63% of the participants have encountered transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The study further reveals that the factors contributing to transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation include long-distance travel to formal healthcare facilities, poor nature of roads, high costs or charges for transportation services and the limited number of commercial vehicles plying rural roads. Studies have identified transportation as a barrier to healthcare utilisation in developing countries (Varela et al., 2019; Agyemang-Duah et al., 2019a). For instance, Agyemang-Duah et al. (2019a) have reported that transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation occur as a result of bad road networks and high transport costs which is synonymous with the findings of this study.
The study finds that it is more likely for the poor older women to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation as compared to the poor older men surviving under the LEAP programme. Thus, this suggests that the poor older women are more likely to experience health disparity resulting from the inability to traverse between dwelling places and the healthcare centres. Poverty, which affects women more than men (Owusu-Afriyie and Nketiah-Amponsah, 2014; Debrah, 2013; Oduro et al., 2011) is likely to be associated with the situation where women are more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. Also, in a situation where women are less likely to own properties such as houses, land, and vehicles, it will be unlikely to even own vehicles and thus access transportation services easily. For instance, a study in Ecuador, Ghana, and India has shown that women are less likely to own properties and/or inherit properties and wealth in Ghana as compared to Ecuador (Deere et al., 2013). Thus, the low wealth base of the women could reflect their ability to prepare for old age including paying for transportation to access formal healthcare services. This situation calls for the need to enact state laws that trump over traditional rites that prevent women from owning and inheriting properties and wealth.
The age group of the poor older people under the LEAP programme also has an association with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. The study finds that respondents aged 85 years or above are significantly more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. This is the least productive group among the poor older people who benefit from the LEAP programme and hence are less likely to undertake economic activities and support themselves financially and afford the cost of transportation services to and from formal healthcare facilities. In most West African Countries, Ogwumike and Aboderin (2005) have identified that older people are at high risk of and more vulnerable to poverty and financial difficulties due to situations or influences that happen earlier or later in life, depleting any or all forms of capital (e.g., human, natural, physical, financial, social and political capital).
Whilst the government is making effort to ensure policies that are likely to improve the financial situation of the poor older people in Ghana, Osei (2011) has argued that affordability has been a huge challenge in providing for these poor older people. However, the policy implication of this research outcome is that genuine and renewed commitment to improving the financial status of the poor older people is needed on the part of the implementers. For instance, studies (e.g., Sackey, 2019; Peprah et al., 2017; Debrah, 2013; Amuzu et al., 2010) have found that vulnerable people under the LEAP programme are still suffering from financial difficulties and thus, there should be more effort to improve the cash transfer amount to better the quality of life of the poor older people.
Most importantly, the results show that participants with a high school education are significantly less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. This study outcome suggests that poor older people who have higher education are more likely to pay for their transportation services. With secondary or high school education (an important form of formal education), people are more employable as compared to people with a lower level of education or no formal education (Adeniran et al., 2020) and consequently, people can contribute to compulsory and voluntary pension schemes for economic sufficiency at old age. The implication of the study outcome is that having some level of formal education is likely to improve the financial status and lessen transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation at old age.
Furthermore, the study outcome reiterates the economic problems associated with the LEAP, a social protection problem meant to improve the economic status of the poor older people. The study findings suggest that poor older people will significantly be able to overcome transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation if they receive a more modest monthly cash transfer. However, only 26% of the participants receive more than 201 Ghana Cedis, the amount of monthly earning that is likely to help the poor older people to escape transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. According to Syed et al. (2013), transportation barriers are mostly experienced by people who depend on low incomes and are not able to afford paid transportation. Thus, the poor older people who are disadvantaged because they are not able to engage in economic activities, are more likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation and as a result, experience poor health outcomes. To improve the quality of life of the poor older people under the LEAP, further measures to improve the financial status will be necessary. Also, the policy implication of the outcome of this study is that there could be initiatives that would ease the financial burdens of older people. For instance, the creation of specialized transportation systems would be a non-financial measure that is likely to ease the financial burden of paid transportation on the poor older people who travel to and from formal healthcare facilities.
6. Conclusion
This objective of the study was to examine the socio-economic and demographic factors associated with transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among the poor older people under the LEAP programme in the Atwima Nwabiagya District of Ghana. The study finds that poor older people 85 or above are more likely to encounter transportation barriers. Also, women are more likely to encounter transportation barriers as compared to men. Economic insufficiency which has been the heart of the LEAP programme determines who encounters transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among the poor older people.
Based on the study outcome, we conclude that beneficiaries of the LEAP programme are less likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation if they are not women. This calls for the empowerment of women at all stages of life or age, especially at the time they could work and save financial resources for old age.
The study concludes that having a higher education level is likely to reduce transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older people. Access to education and training at early stages in life is likely to increase employability skills, ensure access to jobs, and contribution to compulsory and voluntary pension schemes. Thus, this is likely to lead to adequate preparation, financially, towards old age.
Whereas financial problem has been a general issue inhibiting the general functioning of the programme, there are disparities in terms of monthly income earnings and thus, also determines who are likely to encounter transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation. Based on the findings, we conclude that a genuine commitment on the part of social programme implementers to improve the cash transfer amount and ensure regular transfers is likely to lessen the transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among the poor older people.
The analysis of the relationship between public transportation systems and transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation is relevant but beyond the scope of our study. We, therefore, recommend that future research should investigate the correlation between public transportation systems (road networks, distance, means of transportation, public transportation availability and cost of transportation) and transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation among poor older adults in Ghana. Aside from the above, we did not explore gender issues in relation to transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation in detail. Thus, we recommend a detailed exploration of the perspective of gender, social protection and transportation barriers to formal healthcare utilisation in future studies. We further recommend a mixed-methods study that uses questionnaires and in-depth interviews designed in local languages to understand and articulate complex concepts (especially as they relate to their domestic circumstances, livelihoods, poverty, personal and family wellbeing, family support and self-esteem) which are necessary guarantors of accurate, reliable, useful and robust survey results.
