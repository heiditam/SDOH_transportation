Abstract
Previous studies evaluating the social equity of transit-based accessibility to healthcare have failed to consider travel time uncertainty by assuming that transit always operates exactly as scheduled. Their analyses, therefore, tended to ignore travellers’ efforts to avoid being late to their healthcare appointments. These conventional approaches might overestimate accessibility and lead to inaccurate evaluations of social equity. This study aims to investigate how overlooking travellers’ risk-management efforts under travel time uncertainty might result in flawed assessments of equity in transit-based accessibility to healthcare facilities. Specifically, we compare the traditional accessibility approach to the robust space–time accessibility (RSTA) method, which considers travellers’ risk-management strategies (e.g., leaving early, choosing routes based not only on travel time but also travel time variability) depending on whether they are risk-neutral or risk-averse under travel time uncertainty. The RSTA model provides a more conservative, realistic, and user-centred view of transit-based accessibility. To demonstrate this, we conducted an empirical study of Columbus, Ohio, USA, using real-time transit big data. Our results show that traditional measures of accessibility that do not consider the impact of travel time uncertainty cannot accurately capture the social and racial inequity in healthcare accessibility via transit. However, the RSTA method reveals significant inequity in transit-based healthcare accessibility among residents in terms of their socio-economic and demographic status. Moreover, the conventional accessibility method evaluates the new bus rapid transit (BRT) service as promoting the accessibility of disadvantaged people, which the robust accessibility method revealed is not true.

1. Introduction
The importance of social equity in healthcare accessibility – ensuring that individuals can easily access health-shaping resources – cannot be overstated (Neutens, 2015, Penchansky and Thomas, 1981). Research indicates that sub-standard levels of healthcare accessibility can hinder regular clinic visits (Graves, 2008), reduce the use of preventive services (e.g., annual health check-ups) (Fujita et al., 2017), and contribute to chronic and severe health outcomes such as hypertension (Okuyama et al., 2019) and pediatric asthma (Garcia et al., 2015). The COVID-19 pandemic has further underscored the significance of equitable access to healthcare. Studies have suggested that insufficient spatial access to health resources, including testing sites (Mullachery et al., 2022), vaccination sites (D. Liu et al., 2022b), ICU beds (Kang et al., 2020), and medical care (Palm et al., 2021), can exacerbate inequality and inequity in health outcomes.
A key component for achieving social equity in healthcare access is equitable access to healthcare through public transit (Boisjoly et al., 2020) as it is often the primary mode of transportation for socially disadvantaged groups in both the Global North (D. Liu et al., 2022a, Zimmerman, 2005) and South (Sharma and Patil, 2021, Syed et al., 2013) to reach healthcare services. To support evidence-based planning that promotes equity in transit-based access to healthcare, it is crucial to measure accessibility as accurately and realistically as possible. However, many existing studies (El-Geneidy et al., 2016, Farber et al., 2014, Grisé et al., 2019, Kapatsila et al., 2023, Klar et al., 2023, Pereira, 2019, Pereira, 2018, Pereira et al., 2019, Singh et al., 2022, Widener et al., 2015) on transit accessibility are based on unrealistic assumptions (Malekzadeh and Chung, 2020). They often assume that public transit operates perfectly on-time, which is far from reality (L. Liu et al., 2022c, Wessel et al., 2017, Wessel and Farber, 2019). Additionally, most studies presume that transit riders have a single routing objective such as reaching their destinations as quickly as possible through the fastest path. These assumptions can be particularly problematic when measuring healthcare accessibility under travel time uncertainty because travellers may choose alternate routes with less variability in travel time or implement buffer time plans (i.e., leaving early) to avoid being late or missing appointments and incurring late or cancellation fees (Chen et al., 2013, de Palma and Picard, 2005). Addressing travellers’ risk-management behaviours in the face of travel time uncertainty can lead to a more realistic and accurate portrayal of healthcare accessibility and equity.
The overarching goal of this study is to investigate the added value of the robust space–time accessibility (RSTA) method when assessing social equity in public transit access to healthcare under travel time uncertainty. Unlike the traditional accessibility models, the time-geographic RSTA method accounts for transit riders’ risk-management strategies (e.g., buffer time plan, alternate routing choice) based on their risk-taking attitudes (e.g., risk-neutral vs. risk-averse) under travel time uncertainty. We have three objectives. First, using a transit system in the city of Columbus, Ohio, USA, as an example, we will demonstrate how conventional transit accessibility models may overestimate healthcare accessibility by not considering travellers’ diverse risk-management strategies in the face of travel time uncertainty. Second, we will present how this overestimation can lead to an erroneous assessment of social justice in the context of public transit accessibility to healthcare. Lastly, we will examine whether and how neglecting travellers’ heterogeneous buffer time plans and routing strategies for managing risks can distort the evaluation of a new mobility service’s impacts on healthcare access equity.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews previous approaches to measuring spatial accessibility to healthcare and measuring accessibility to urban resources under travel time uncertainty. The section also provides background information about the RSTA and traditional accessibility (TA) models which will be used in this research. We then describe our study design, setting, and data sources. In that same section, we also elaborate on how we generated accessibility metrics and conducted social equity analysis. Section 4 reports the results of our analysis. The final section offers a summary, limitations, and policy implications of this study.
2. Background
2.1. Literature review
2.1.1. Approaches to measuring geographic accessibility to healthcare
Accessibility is a fundamental concept in the transportation, social, urban sciences (Geurs and van Wee, 2004, Handy and Niemeier, 1997, Levinson and Wu, 2020, Siddiq and Taylor, 2021). Its importance has been emphasized in the public health domain as well given the role of spatial accessibility to healthcare in health disparities (Kelly et al., 2016). Various approaches, spanning from the simple distance metric to one with multiple catchment areas, have been used to measure healthcare accessibility and its social equity (Higgs, 2004, McLafferty, 2003, Neutens, 2015). Brabyn and Skelly (2002) revealed the disparities in accessibility to public hospitals in northern and southern parts of New Zealand. They base their findings on the road network-based distance and travel time from the census enumeration district to the nearest hospital. These measures were considered more sophisticated than Euclidean distance-based, container (e.g., the number of healthcare facilities within a neighbourhood), and coverage (e.g., the number of healthcare services within a 1 km buffer from a population centroid) approaches (Higgs, 2004, Talen, 2003).
However, these proximity-based measures fail to consider the competition between individuals at healthcare locations considering the dynamics of supply (e.g., the number of doctors) and demand. Population-to-practitioner ratios or regional availability approaches were proposed to address this limitation by comparing supply and demand within a defined area (Joseph and Phillips, 1984). This method, however, has two shortcomings. One flaw is that it assumes that people only use healthcare facilities within the defined area. There are no cross-boundary flows of people. The other limitation is that it ignores the effects of distance decay and applies constant weight to healthcare within the area, even though people usually prefer using nearby healthcare facilities to more distant ones.
The two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) approach (Luo and Wang, 2003) enhanced with a distance decay function (Luo and Qi, 2009) addresses both limitations. Researchers have been developing and applying this 2SFCA method to investigate disparities in healthcare accessibility in a more realistic manner. For instance, instead of relying on the conventional 2SFCA approach based on a single transport mode, Mao and Nekorchuk, (2013) evaluated and synthesised spatial accessibility by multiple transportation modes to consider people’s travel behaviours (e.g., using public transit instead of private automobile) under various circumstances (e.g., car-free population, living in highly congested areas). The authors found that the multi-mode 2SFCA better captures the disparity in healthcare accessibility between rural and urban areas without under- or overestimation of accessibility.
The COVID-19 pandemic has expanded the use of the 2SFCA in measuring healthcare accessibility with a social equity lens. Using the parallel enhanced 2SFCA method via powerful CyberGIS resources, Kang et al., (2020) examined whether at-risk populations in Illinois, USA, have equitable spatial accessibility to ICU beds and ventilators. Similar research (Kim et al., 2021) has measured spatial accessibility to ICU beds, identified areas with shortages of accessibility, and found associations between areas of low accessibility and socio-economic/demographic variables in Florida, USA. To avoid overestimating local demand, the 3SFCA approach (Wan et al., 2012) was used in the paper instead. In the Global South, Pereira et al., (2021) uncovered substantial income, racial, and spatial inequalities in geographic access to COVID-19 healthcare resources by using the balanced floating catchment area method (Paez et al., 2019) that takes into account the effects of competition and congestion when measuring accessibility.
Despite their utility, all of these measures of healthcare accessibility are unrealistic, because they were measured within the transportation networks that did not take traffic jams or other unforeseeable delays into account. Section 2.1.2. reviews studies on measuring accessibility under travel time uncertainty.
2.1.2. Measuring spatial accessibility under travel time uncertainty
When measuring accessibility under travel time uncertainty, time-geographic accessibility measures of space-time prisms (STPs) (Hägerstrand, 1970, Miller, 2005, Miller, 1991) have been particularly popular in capturing travellers’ risk-management behaviours such as planning buffer time, or choosing a route with less variability in travel time (Durán-Hormazábal and Tirachini, 2016, USDOT-FHA, 2023) to arrive at their destinations on time. One of the first attempts was by Ettema and Timmermans (2007). Building upon the space-time utility framework (Miller, 1999), they introduced new time-geographic accessibility measures that consider several factors affecting accessibility under travel time uncertainty, such as 1) individuals’ ability to adapt their activity patterns when faced with limited choice sets; 2) variation in individuals’ perception of travel times; 3) the temporal fluctuation of travel times; and 4) the effect of travel time information on accessibility. A key underlying premise of the research was that travellers would respond to travel time uncertainty in different ways and to different degrees because they have different traits, social roles, residential/work locations, and access to mobility options.
Chen et al., (2013) elaborated on this reasoning with the development of reliable STPs. Reliable STPs delineate the potential activity space of individuals by explicitly considering their different levels of concern for on-time arrival. The study showed that the space-time extent of people’s accessible areas can change because they can budget extra time to manage risks, depending on their different concerns about the probability of arriving on time (e.g., 50% vs. 95%). Liao et al., (2014) offered an alternative to the reliable STP model by incorporating activity-travel time uncertainty and stochastic space-time prisms into multistate super networks for evaluating accessibility. The applications of the reliable STP concept have been expanded to the social equity analysis of accessibility. Chen et al., (2019) demonstrated that people’s on-time arrival constraints can affect the social equity analysis of accessibility by showing that accessibility measures based on deterministic travel time assumptions can lead to significant bias in assessments of accessibility equity. The reliable STP framework was also integrated into the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method as a means to evaluate geographic access to healthcare by considering supply (e.g., number of beds/doctors) and demand (e.g., population) components as well as on-time arrival constraints (Chen et al., 2020).
Despite the progress in the literature of healthcare accessibility studies under travel time uncertainty, two major gaps remain. One is that transit-based accessibility and its social equity under travel time uncertainty have been largely overlooked. Most studies on reliability-based accessibility measurements focus on automobile travel (e.g., via taxi GPS tracking data) (Chen et al., 2017, Chen et al., 2013). Only a handful of studies (Conway et al., 2018, Fu et al., 2022; L. Liu et al., 2022c, Zhang et al., 2018) account for travel time uncertainty when measuring public transit-based accessibility. Zhang et al., (2018) extended the reliable STP model by Chen et al., 2017, Chen et al., 2013 to the public transit network and generated varying sizes of isochrone maps based on different on-time arrival probabilities using automatic vehicle location data in Shenzhen, China. More recently, people’s interaction potential (e.g., shopping or dining together) based on their joint STPs within the transit network under travel time uncertainty was measured using massive smart card data in Nanjing, China (Fu et al., 2022). This relative lack of attention naturally led to a dearth of research on effects of the travel time uncertainty on the social equity of transit-based accessibility.
The other, but more important gap, is that a comprehensive consideration of people’s risk-management strategies under travel time uncertainty is needed. Travellers can react to travel time uncertainty in two ways: 1) giving themselves a safety margin (i.e., buffer time); and 2) choosing routes with less travel time variability where travel time variability can be measured as variation (e.g., standard deviation) of transit or road travel time (Durán-Hormazábal and Tirachini, 2016, USDOT-FHA, 2023). However, previous research either for automobile or transit travel often considers the buffer time plan only. Travel time variability-based routing as another risk-management strategy for travellers in the face of travel time uncertainty has been largely neglected when measuring accessibility.
Lee and Miller (2020) fill this gap by explicitly considering both buffer time planning and routing strategy when evaluating transit-based healthcare accessibility under travel time uncertainty. They also take into account how people’s heterogeneous risk-taking attitudes are reflected in their buffer time plans and routing strategies, thereby generating different accessibility outcomes. Their case study in Columbus, Ohio, demonstrates that the number of accessible healthcare services by public transit can decrease or increase up to approximately 3,400, depending on a person’s risk-taking attitude in the face of travel time uncertainty. This fluctuation can prevent policymakers and planners from accurately assessing the accessibility landscape and rationally allocating health-shaping resources to truly needy areas. To overcome this limitation, Lee and Miller (2020) proposed robust space–time accessibility (RSTA): geographic areas where individuals can reach activity locations regardless of their heterogeneous buffer time plans and navigating strategies. The robust accessibility framework can provide a conservative, reasonable, user-centred view of accessibility under travel time uncertainty. Given the significant impact of travel time uncertainty on the social equity of accessibility (Chen et al., 2019), this robust accessibility method with comprehensive consideration of travellers’ different risk-management strategies can facilitate a more accurate and rigorous analysis of transit-based accessibility equity under travel time uncertainty.
2.2. Robust vs. traditional accessibility model within public transit networks
This section provides a brief description of the robust vs. the traditional accessibility model within public transit networks as a background for the paper. For more details, interested readers can consult the original method paper (Lee and Miller, 2020).
Robust space–time accessibility (RSTA) – geographic areas that are accessible regardless of different buffer time plans as well as route navigating strategies – are measured based upon the concept of STPs in time geography. An STP is a three-dimensional envelope of all possible paths connecting an origin and a destination given individuals’ constraints (e.g., time budget, mobility options, activity schedule) in space and time (Hägerstrand, 1970, Miller, 2005, Miller, 1991). The STP is a sensitive individual-level accessibility measure that can highlight differences in accessibility among people with different perceptions of travel time uncertainty (Ettema and Timmermans, 2007). Leveraging this STP’s strength, RSTA measures can capture the difference in accessibility among travellers with varying buffer time plans and routing strategy combinations under travel time uncertainty.
The main difference between the RSTA and the conventional accessibility models within public transit networks is how the routing problem is formulated. Given a static transit network without any negative (i.e., early arrival) or positive (i.e., delay) deviations from schedules, most traditional transit-based accessibility studies formulate their routing problem with a single-objective one: the shortest (based on distance) or fastest (based on travel time) pathfinding problem (Malekzadeh and Chung, 2020). In contrast, the RSTA method frames its routing problem as a multi-objective routing problem, recognising that passengers would prefer to reach their destinations both quickly and reliably under travel time uncertainty to avoid missing their healthcare appointments. The outcomes of the multi-objective routing problem are Pareto-optimal (or non-dominated) routes, which are the starting point of computing RSTA measures.
To find the Pareto-optimal transit routes in terms of travel time and travel time variability, the RSTA model adopts the fast non-dominated sorting algorithm (FNSA) from the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) (Deb et al., 2002). As an initial step of FNSA, a set of route alternatives from an origin stop to a destination stop is obtained via the k-shortest path algorithm developed by Martins et al., (1999). Two types of weights – average and standard deviation of link travel time – are used to secure various routing alternatives representing travellers’ heterogeneous route choices. Route alternatives are then ranked based on three attributes: 1) faster travel time; 2) less travel time variability (Durán-Hormazábal and Tirachini, 2016, USDOT-FHA, 2023); and 3) fewer transfers. Here, transit travel time consists of walking time, waiting time, and effective in-vehicle time – which is the sum of the expected in-vehicle travel time and the buffer time (i.e., safety margin) – to secure given on-time arrival probability. The next step is to generate a set of six potential path areas (PPAs) – a projection of STPs onto a two-dimensional geographic space (Miller, 1991) – to represent accessibility based on every possible combination of two buffer time plans with different on-time arrival probability concerns (e.g., 50%, 95%) and three transit routing strategies (e.g., fastest, least travel time variability, fewest transfers) described above.
Subsequently, a set of six PPAs is composited and intersected. Like the intersected set in mathematics, the intersected area of the PPAs is geographic areas that are accessible regardless of different risk-management attitudes in buffer time planning and route navigation strategy. As a last step, the number of opportunities (e.g., healthcare, job, food) within a robust PPA is counted and used as a final RSTA metric.

3. Methods
3.1. Study design, setting, and data sources
This study is a comparative study of social equity evaluation results driven by RSTA versus TA measures using the city of Columbus, Ohio, USA, as a study area. As a medium-sized city in the Midwest, Columbus has often been used as a living lab in which researchers test new concepts and methodologies in urban analytics (Can, 1990), human mobility (Kwan, 1999), neighbourhood dynamics (Lee et al., 2021), and public health (Hyder et al., 2021). Columbus consists of 589 Census Block Groups (CBGs). A CBG is an administrative unit defined by the United States Census Bureau. It includes 600 to 3000 residents, and it is the finest spatial unit that provides important sociodemographic variables (e.g., income level, race/ethnicity, gender) that are crucial for equity analysis.
We study social equity in transit-based accessibility to healthcare services in those CBGs with three interrelated objectives: Objective 1 illustrates how the TA measures tend to overestimate accessibility by overlooking travellers’ heterogenous risk-management strategies under travel time uncertainty; Objective 2 presents how this overestimation of accessibility can lead to an erroneous evaluation of social equity in terms of transit-based accessibility to healthcare; and Objective 3 examines how ignoring individuals’ heterogenous buffer time plans and navigation strategies in the face of travel time uncertainty can result in a misleading assessment of the impact of a new public transit service on equity in healthcare accessibility.
For Objective 3, we consider a new BRT system called CMAX, introduced in Columbus in January 2018. CMAX is a response to adverse socio-economic (e.g., high unemployment rate) and health outcomes (e.g., high infant mortality rate) in underserved neighbourhoods in Columbus due to the lack of accessibility to urban opportunities and facilities (Bliss, 2016). The goal of the CMAX BRT system is to increase transit-based accessibility to jobs and healthcare by connecting citizens to the clusters of employment (e.g., downtown, shopping malls) and health-shaping resources (Central Ohio Transit Authority, 2022). A series of studies have presented the positive effects of the new BRT service on ridership (Lee and Miller, 2022), individual-level access (Lee and Miller, 2018) and accessibility equity (Lee and Miller, 2019) via static transport networks without any delays. The assessment of reliable accessibility based on stochastic transit networks with changing travel times also showed an overall increase in accessibility in underserved neighbourhoods after CMAX began operation (Lee and Miller, 2020). However, it is unclear whether the accessibility benefits thanks to the new BRT system have been distributed equitably. This research attempts to complete the puzzle by examining whether the new BRT service would still be an accessibility equaliser even under travel time uncertainty. More broadly, the results will advance methodologies to evaluate the social equity of transit-based accessibility to health-shaping resources under travel time uncertainty and facilitate evidence-based decision-making to improve access to and equity of healthcare.
We obtained and used static GTFS files of the Central Ohio Transit Authority (COTA) through the OpenMobilityData website (http://transitfeeds.com), which is the archive of transit schedules around the world. The Peartree Python package (Butts, 2022) was used to convert static GTFS files into routable public transit networks. The September 3, 2017, version (covers September 4 to December 31, 2017) and September 3, 2018, version (covers September 3 to January 6, 2019) of static GTFS files were used to create a transit network for pre- and post-new BRT construction, respectively. We also collected real-time GTFS data for computing empirical travel time distributions. Real-time GTFS data provides crucial transit operational information including vehicle positions and trip updates in real-time (Google, 2022). We collected real-time GTFS data for December 2017 (for the pre-BRT period) and 2018 (for the post-BRT period) through the COTA’s Application Programming Interface (API) at a one-minute resolution using Python and archived it in MongoDB databases for analysis. For the locations of healthcare services, we utilised geocoded healthcare services data (North American Industry Classification System code 62, excluding social assistance businesses) from Data Axle Business Data (https://www.data-axle.com/our-data/business-data).
3.2. Accessibility metrics
Following the procedures described in Section 2.2 (Background), we computed RSTA and TA metrics in our study area. Specifically, we created a hexagon-based robust PPA and classic PPA from each origin (i.e., transit stop), computed the number of healthcare services within robust PPA and classic PPA, and use the resulting numbers as an RSTA and TA metric for the origin, respectively. The size (length of each side) of a hexagon grid (i.e., the service area of a transit stop) is 0.35 miles, reflecting empirical survey results on the walking distance to bus stops (El-Geneidy et al., 2014). When computing transit travel time, walking time is calculated based on the average walking speed of 4.5 km per hour based on the literature that reviewed a wide range of walking speeds used in transportation studies (Stępniak and Goliszek, 2017).
As for delineating PPAs, we considered the following specifications. All transit stops within the transit system were used as trip origins, and a 90-minute travel time budget was considered since it is often used as the maximum threshold of space-time constrained cumulative opportunity measures when evaluating transit-based accessibility and changes in accessibility before and after the introduction of new transit services (Klar et al., 2023). Also, a total of 20 route alternatives (10 based on average link travel time as weight, and 10 based on the standard deviation of link travel time as weight) between an origin-destination pair were considered to identify the Pareto route that was fastest, had the least travel time variability, and the fewest transfers for generating PPAs. We computed the mean value of travel times from an origin stop to the stops within a hexagon and utilised the resulting average value as the travel time between the origin stop and the hexagon to delineate PPAs. Morning peak time (from 6:30 AM to 9:00 AM) on weekdays was used as a departure time as well as a data collection period to calculate empirical travel time distributions based on real-time GTFS data.
As a preparation for the social equity analysis at the neighbourhood level in the next section, we calculated an average RSTA as well as TA value of all transit stops located in a CBG. We aggregated the stop-level accessibility into the neighbourhood-level accessibility to utilise detailed information on various sociodemographic variables that are available at the CBG level (e.g., American Community Survey 2018 5-year estimates) for social equity analysis of accessibility using spatial regression models in section 3.3.
3.3. Social equity evaluation
3.3.1. Gini coefficients
Before diving into the social equity assessment using spatial regression models, we first use Gini coefficients to obtain an overview of healthcare accessibility inequity based on the TA and RSTA methods. The Gini coefficient is an effective and commonly used approach in accessibility equity research thanks to its easy interpretation through the use of Lorenz curves (Cheng et al., 2020, Lucas et al., 2016). The Gini coefficient of 1 indicates a perfect inequality in healthcare accessibility whereas the Gini coefficient of 0 indicates a complete equality in healthcare accessibility. Although the Gini coefficient is an effective measure of equity, a single number between 0 and 1 cannot paint a full picture of the accessibility equity landscape. For instance, the Gini coefficient cannot tell us which population group is showing a lower or higher level of accessibility. To this end, we use spatial regression to see the association between accessibility measures and socio-economic/demographic variables. Detailed descriptions of the spatial regression models employed in this study are provided in section 3.3.2.

4. Results
4.1. Neighbourhood-level accessibility maps based on the traditional vs. robust accessibility model
Fig. 2 presents spatial distributions of the neighbourhood-level healthcare accessibility based on the TA (Fig. 2a) and RSTA (Fig. 2b) methods. The figure illustrates that the TA model considerably overestimates the neighbourhood-level accessibility to healthcare in the overall study area. On the contrary, the RSTA model demonstrates a more conservative picture of healthcare accessibility. In other words, the results suggest that the conventional accessibility method, which overlooks passengers’ heterogeneous risk-taking attitudes in buffer time plans and routing strategy, can be inaccurate. These results are based on the transit network with the BRT service. In the next section, we explain how this overestimation of accessibility can lead to an erroneous equity evaluation of public transit accessibility to healthcare.

4.2. Equity analysis results using Gini coefficients
Fig. 3 provides the Gini coefficients of TA and RSTA as well as the Lorenz curves based on them. The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative distribution of CBGs on the horizontal axis, and the cumulative distribution of the indicator (e.g., accessibility) on the vertical axis. The Gini coefficient is calculated as the ratio of the area between the equal distribution line and the Lorenz curve to the area under the triangle formed by the equal distribution line, the horizontal axis, and the vertical axis. The Gini index of the TA (0.256) is lower than the Gini coefficient of the robust accessibility (0.384). This indicates that the TA model evaluates the distribution of healthcare accessibility within the study area as more equitable than the RSTA model does. The Lorenz line of traditional accessibility, which is closer to the perfect equal distribution line corroborates that the TA model paints a rosier picture in terms of the accessibility equity than the RSTA model does. The results imply that using the TA method might lead to an incorrect evaluation of the equity impacts of the new BRT route on transit-based healthcare accessibility. When we utilise the TA method (Model 3), the result indicates that the healthcare accessibility of CBGs with a higher percentage of non-White, seniors, and unemployed people increased significantly after the new BRT service was introduced. From the social equity perspective, the Model 3 result implies that the new BRT system might mitigate the inequity in healthcare accessibility because the new route benefits disadvantaged and vulnerable people – racial minorities, unemployed people, and seniors – who largely rely on public transit (Anderson, 2023, Burrows et al., 2021, Taylor and Morris, 2015). In other words, the new BRT might be an effective public policy as it contributes to promoting equity.
However, we argue that our Model 4 results (which are based on the RSTA method) challenge the finding obtained from Model 3. Model 4 results indicate that racial minorities, unemployed people, and older people did not experience improvement in transit-based healthcare accessibility after the introduction of the new BRT service. This result implies that the introduction of the BRT did not necessarily lead to better transit-based healthcare accessibility for disadvantaged people. This suggests that the policy evaluation of the impact of the new BRT service on transit-based healthcare accessibility might be erroneous when the evaluation is based on the TA measure, which does not address travel time uncertainty impacts or travellers’ risk-management strategies based on their different risk-taking attitudes. This can be problematic when formulating policies because the inaccurate evaluation of the new BRT service’s impacts on transit equity can lead to the misallocation of public resources.
5. Conclusion
An imperative first step to promoting social equity in transit-based accessibility to healthcare is to measure accessibility as realistically and accurately as possible. With the development of new spatial and temporal high-resolution transit data, there has been growing interest in studying the social equity of transit-based accessibility in relation to healthcare. However, most studies rely on simplistic but unrealistic assumptions. Transit service is assumed to follow a strict schedule, without any delays or early arrivals. Put another way, traditional transit-based accessibility studies have not considered the factor of travel time uncertainty. Naturally, passengers’ heterogeneous risk-management strategies to account for this uncertainty and arrive at their healthcare appointments on time have also been overlooked. In a transit network without any dynamism, travellers presumably do not give themselves buffer time and always take the fastest path, although traffic conditions can make it unreliable. These assumptions can lead to the overestimation of accessibility to healthcare and the misdiagnosis of the landscape of equity in cities’ healthcare accessibility.
This paper alerts researchers and practitioners in the fields of transport and health to the importance of considering the impact of travel time uncertainty when evaluating transit-based health access equity. We demonstrated the added value of the RSTA model in uncovering the reality of transit-based healthcare access and its social equity. Unlike most TA models, the novel RSTA model explicitly considers travel time uncertainty and individuals’ combinations of buffer time plan and route finding to avoid the risk of arriving late to destinations.
However, our research has several limitations that future studies can address. First, computing the RSTA model requires researchers to conduct more extensive data processing and computing processes than traditional methods do. This may impose an extra burden on researchers and policymakers whose resources are limited, such as small metropolitan planning organisations or low- and middle-income countries (Kim et al., 2023, Kim et al., 2022). We plan to develop another RSTA model that is less data- and computing-intensive and thus can be applied to study areas with limited resources. Second, we utilised the neighbourhood-level accessibility that was aggregated at the CBG level. Different aggregation methods might produce different results (Javanmard et al., 2023, Kwan, 2012). Future studies can benefit from conducting sensitivity analyses to yield more robust results. Also, another viable direction for future research is to develop a reliability-based two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method within public transit networks to consider the competition effects or supply-demand dynamics when measuring spatial accessibility, which was not addressed in this study. Next, this study simplified people’s risk-taking attitudes into two types: risk-neutral and risk-averse. A more comprehensive consideration of individuals’ risk-taking attitudes would facilitate a more nuanced policy recommendation. For instance, a follow-up study can conduct a sensitivity analysis based on a wider range of on-time arrival probabilities (e.g., 50%, 70%, 90%) to investigate the extent to which the accessibility values can change depending on the on-time arrival probability used. Lastly, we assumed a normal distribution of inter-stop travel times based on the literature. However, we acknowledge that this might not always be the case. Future research could modify this assumption to more accurately measure transit accessibility in the context of travel time uncertainty.
Healthy city planning and policy efforts require accurate and realistic measures of accessibility in order to allocate resources effectively and optimally to areas in the greatest need (Luo, 2004, Luo and Whippo, 2012, Rosero-Bixby, 2004). Without these improved measures, healthcare and transport planners may miss areas that are underserved and deliver less effective mitigation policies and intervention programmes. In this study, we found that the TA model tends to overestimate transit-based accessibility to healthcare services. This overestimation can lead to truly needy neighbourhoods being overlooked. Accessibility results based on the RSTA model showed a more conservative outcome. Our spatial regression models demonstrated that the overestimation of accessibility by the TA model has led to an erroneous evaluation of the social equity of transit-based accessibility to healthcare. Moreover, our study provides empirical evidence that overlooking travel time uncertainty and travellers’ heterogeneous reactions to it can distort the equity impact assessment of new public transit services. As a more practical, realistic, and user-centred accessibility measure, the RSTA model will allow the provision of more effective and efficient use of limited resources to truly needy areas, thereby promoting social equity within urban areas.
