Abstract
Prehospital emergency medical services play a vital role in reducing mortality and disease burden in low- and middle-income countries. However, the availability of adequate prehospital emergency care remains a significant challenge in many resource-limited communities, with over 91% of the African population lacking access to sufficient emergency medical services. This commentary aims to highlight the critical components of transportation infrastructure and medical supply chain challenges for emergency medical service development and propose potential solutions for future study. Transportation is a key factor influencing prehospital mortality, yet many low- and middle-income countries face issues related to timely prehospital transportation, with patients often relying on family members or private vehicles for transportation, leading to delays in reaching healthcare facilities due to poor road infrastructure. Dysfunctional and inadequate vehicles are also common barriers to timely care. Response times and transport times often exceed high-income standards, with some rural areas experiencing total prehospital time, defined as the time of injury to arrival at definitive care, exceeding 24 hours. To address these transportation challenges, some low- and middle-income countries have developed tier-1 emergency medical services programs that use existing transportation infrastructure and involve lay first responders using motorized and non-motorized vehicles. These programs prioritize rapid transportation over advanced on-scene intervention, potentially providing faster response times.
A combination of tier-1 and tier-2 emergency medical services systems, as seen in some successful examples, allows for early on-scene guidance and resource allocation. In addition to transportation, the availability of medical equipment is crucial for effective prehospital interventions, particularly in tier-2 systems. However, low- and middle-income countries often face shortages of even basic supplies, limiting the scope of care that emergency medical services personnel can provide. Developing tier-2 emergency medical services upon a foundation of tier-1 prehospital care utilizing sustainable local supply chains and common household items for basic care can help alleviate these equipment challenges. The integration of tier-1 and tier-2 systems may offer a promising solution to address resource limitations and improve timely access to emergency care in low- and middle-income countries. Further research and investment are required to explore and implement these solutions, ultimately reducing mortality and enhancing healthcare services in resource-limited communities.

Introduction
Prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) may alleviate up to 36% of mortality and 45% of the total disease burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1; however, many resource-limited communities lack sufficient prehospital emergency care. In fact, a recent study has shown that over 91% of the African population lacks access to adequate EMS.2 As discussed throughout this series, these systems rely on a complex architecture of healthcare personnel, transportation and dispatch infrastructure, and established supply chains, enabling access to medical supplies. While each of these individual components is, in itself, necessary to support sustainable EMS, no system, regardless of its size or scope, can provide sufficient patient care without all 3. As cost has been frequently cited as the largest barrier to EMS implementation in LMICs, the requirements of both reliable transportation infrastructure and medical supply contribute greatly to the deficit of services described in resource-limited settings.3 However, as with certification requirements for EMS personnel described elsewhere in this series, there exists no global consensus on frameworks for either transportation infrastructure or medical supply chain development in LMICs. As such, the present commentary will seek to outline challenges identified in transportation and resource availability among EMS programs in LMICs as well as to review several proposed solutions for future development.
Transportation Needs for EMS Development in LMICs
Transport time has been widely noted as one of the most important predictors of prehospital mortality in high-income settings and has been shown to be a major influence on mortality in undifferentiated trauma patients. While the literature base describing the importance of rapid transport is far more robust amongst high-income EMS programs, several small-scale studies have similarly demonstrated its importance for both trauma and cardiac emergencies in LMICs.4 Given that disease pathophysiology remains unchanged between geographical contexts, there is also no reason to conclude that rapid transport is not equally, if not more important, in resource-limited settings. However, a recent systematic review of prehospital emergency care in LMICs by Bhattarai et al noted that a vast number of programs report issues related to transport care regarding prehospital emergency care. Included studies found that patients often lacked formal prehospital transportation and were most frequently transported by family members or private vehicles rather than ambulances. In addition, patients reported delays in reaching health facilities due to difficult terrain and poor road infrastructure, leading to poor outcomes.4 EMS programs in sub-Saharan Africa also frequently report dysfunctioning vehicles and an inadequate number of vehicles as a barrier to care.5 The reason for the inadequate vehicles can be multifactorial and include reasons such as lack of financial means, inability to maintain or service vehicles, and the inability to staff the vehicles that have been allocated to EMS. As such, reported response times among 22 programs in LMICs ranged between ranged from 6.6 to 24.2 minutes, with transport times ranging between 7.2 and 83.5 minutes,4 drastically exceeding high-income standards total prehospital time. In some rural settings, total prehospital times have been noted to exceed 24 hours.6
Transportation Solutions for EMS Development in LMICs
Despite its challenges, many prehospital EMS programs in LMICs have demonstrated unique solutions to providing sustainable transport for injured and ill patients. Communities in East Asia and the Pacific, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, where commercial ambulances are often not readily available, have developed tier-1 EMS programs leveraging pre-existing transportation infrastructure via the training of lay first responders on both motorized and non-motorized vehicles.7 These programs prioritize rapid transportation over advanced on-scene intervention and may be supported by evidence suggesting that advanced life support care imparts minimal benefit over basic life support care in the prehospital setting. Tier-1 systems utilizing pre-existing infrastructure may also benefit from the following 3 key tenets: (1) transportation providers are inherently self-dispersing, (2) transportation providers already possess their own means of transportation, and (3) transportation providers tend to congregate in areas of high trauma volume. Despite these advantages, lay first responder programs that rely on pre-existing transportation infrastructure are limited by a lack of formal dispatch infrastructure, relying solely on publicly visible encounters such as road traffic injuries. They may also be limited by the mode of transportation, as many programs describe bicycle or motorcycle transport, preventing the transportation of critically ill or injured patients.7
A more holistic solution may involve some combination of tier-1 and tier-2 EMS systems. While the literature is sparse with examples of this implementation, some, such as the collaboration between the South African Tier-1 Emergency First Aid Responder program and Tier-2 Western Cape Government Health and Wellness EMS, as well as a Tanzanian program training both professional and lay first responders, have demonstrated its feasibility in LMICs.8 Despite the education, equipment, and transportation limitations of tier-1 first responders, combined systems allow for early on-scene guidance for triage, navigation, and resource allocation of tier-2 responders. Tier-1 responders may also have faster response times as motorcycles, bicycles, or tricycles more easily navigate through crowded city centers, allowing personnel to provide life-saving stabilization of critical patients before the arrival of more traditional ambulances for transport.9 In fact, a recent study simulating the dispatch of tier-1 responders in urban settings to augment prehospital emergency response found an average response time of only 5.65 minutes along a 10 km stretch of highway.10 Tier-1 responders in this model may also provide responses to less acute cases and perform more general public health interventions, including post-discharge follow-up, further freeing more advanced tier-2 vehicles to cover a larger area.
Equipment Needs for EMS Development in LMICs
Though perhaps not superseding the importance of timely transportation, adequate medical equipment supply has also been shown to improve outcomes associated with prehospital interventions, most notably those provided via professional (tier-2) systems for advanced life support.7 Disease control priorities note that the importance of adequate medical supplies for EMS personnel is two-fold; in addition to allowing first responders to provide care limited only by their scope of practice, sufficient supplies enable education programs to expand their scope of training.7 Unfortunately, the resource burden of traditional “Western” tier-2 EMS systems may make them less financially viable for LMICs. Tier-2 programs require substantially more single-use resources than informal tier-1 EMS. Tier-2 systems most often demand the availability of equipment for more advanced measures of life support, such as endotracheal intubation, intravenous cannulation, cardiac monitoring, and defibrillation, as well as a variety of analgesic, sedative, cardiac, and neuromodulating medications. However, several recent reviews have noted the dearth of even basic supply for prehospital emergency care in LMICs. Scoping reviews of airway management and spinal cord immobilization among first responders in LMICs found that, while training programs provided instruction for both airway management and spinal immobilization, only 35.3% and 21.7%, respectively, possessed adequate equipment to perform this as a basic life support standard of care.11 Similarly, Bhattarai et al noted the lack of equipment alongside the lack of functioning dedicated vehicles as a nearly universal impediment to prehospital emergency care in LMICs.4
Equipment Solutions for EMS Development in LMICs
Despite the large breadth of trauma that may be addressed via tier-1 EMS systems, these programs generally have limited resource requirements. Tier-1 first responders are often equipped with first aid kits that can be created or replenished with common or household items (Table I).12 The use of only basic medical supplies makes quickly identifying local vendors and developing robust supply chains for distribution feasible and inexpensive regardless of setting, allowing for rapid program scaling and the proliferation of basic care and transportation services. Community partnerships with local vendors for the continuous supply of basic materials also alleviate reliance on imported medical equipment that may not be locally available in austere environments. Additionally, novel solutions have also been developed with household items, such as five studies that report tier-1 programs utilizing bath towels as c-spine immobilization,11 which may allow tier-1 responders to produce first aid kits of their own when provided with adequate reimbursement. Given the ease with which tier-1 systems avoid many resource limitations commonly described as a limited factor for deployment of tier-2 EMS by leveraging local knowledge and available resources, it is recommended that nascent EMS programs establish tier-1 services as a foundation upon which Tier-2 care may be realized. During the subsequent development of tier-2 systems from established tier-1 prehospital emergency care, implementers should first prioritize the identification and expansion of sustainable local supply chains and establish protocols for the procurement of all required medical consumables and equipment before establishing or scaling up operations.

In conclusion, an adequate supply of appropriately functioning vehicles and medical equipment poses a significant barrier to EMS development in LMICs, limiting transportation abilities and application of first responder training, respectively. Given a broad literature base supporting tier-1 interventions, future investment is merited to integrate tier-1 and tier-2 systems, alleviating resource burdens borne by low-income communities and permitting more timely access to emergency care for a broader subset of patients.
