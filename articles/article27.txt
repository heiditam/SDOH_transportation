Abstract
Objective
Drug-related arrests persist at high levels despite the negative health consequences of incarceration, particularly for those with a substance use disorder. To understand community-level factors potentially contributing to high rates of drug-related arrests, we conducted an ecological study estimating associations between county-level social determinants of health and rates of drug-related charges in North Carolina from 2016 to 2019.
Method
Allowing a one-year lag between variables, we regressed the county-level rates of drug-related charges on each county-level social determinant of health variable. County-level rates of drug-related charges were the number of drug-related charges per 1000 residents. The county-level social determinant of health variables describe the percent of the population experiencing poverty, unemployment, evictions, food insecurity, or uninsured status. We accounted for spatial dependence of the social determinants of health variables and spatial correlation of the errors. We report direct effects, which measure the effect of a given social determinant of health in a given county on rates of drug-related charges in that same county.
Results
Poverty, evictions, and food insecurity were not associated with county-level rates of drug-related charges. There was a positive association between both unemployment and drug-related charges and between lack of health insurance and drug-related charges. A one percentage point increase in county-level unemployment was associated with a 3.27 (95 % CI: 1.28, 5.26) percent increase in the rate of next year drug-related charges per 1000 adults. A one percentage point increase in county-level lack of health insurance was associated with a 110.50 (95 % CI: 52.81, 168.18) percent increase in drug-related charges per 1000 adults the following year.
Conclusion
In this descriptive analysis, we found that a higher percentage of the population unemployed and uninsured were associated with higher rates of drug-related charges in the subsequent year. Areas with high unemployment and a high percentage of the population uninsured may benefit from diversion programs for individuals with substance use disorders.

Rates of drug overdose deaths have steadily and rapidly increased in recent years. In North Carolina from 2010 to 2019, the rate of drug overdose deaths increased 100 %, from 11.2 to 22.4 per 100,000 population (NC Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.). The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated this trend with a 72 % increase in the overdose death rate from 2019 to 2021 in the state (NC Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.). Amid this worsening overdose crisis, drug-related arrests remained at high levels. In 2019, over 1.5 million people were arrested for drug-related reasons across the United States (U.S.), more than any other crime category (Pew Charitable Trusts, n.d.). These drug-related arrests occur disproportionately among Black individuals (Pew Charitable Trusts, n.d.).
Arrest and incarceration are associated with multiple negative health outcomes. Arrest and incarceration can create barriers to treatment for those with substance use disorders (SUDs) and are associated with an increased risk of drug overdose death (Krawczyk et al., 2017; Ranapurwala et al., 2018). While 63 % of individuals sentenced to jail in the U.S. have a SUD, only about 22 % receive treatment (National Academies of Sciences, E. and M, 2019). Individuals leaving incarceration are at increased risk of opioid overdose death (Binswanger et al., 2013; Ranapurwala et al., 2018; Ranapurwala et al., 2022). In North Carolina, formerly incarcerated persons are >46 times as likely to have a fatal opioid overdose within the first two weeks of release compared to the general population (Ranapurwala et al., 2022).
Despite high levels of drug-related arrests and known health-related risks of arrest and incarceration for individuals with a SUD, there is little information on community-level predictors of drug-related charge rates. Understanding community-level factors associated with higher rates of drug-related charges may help inform broader social and structural interventions with potential for widespread reach. Our primary aim was to estimate the associations between several county-level social determinants of health and rates of drug-related charges in North Carolina using pre-COVID-19 pandemic data, given pandemic-related changes in arrests, incarceration, and economic conditions. In secondary analyses, we added data from the COVID-19 pandemic years to examine these associations throughout a longer and more recent period.
1. Methods
We performed an ecological study using charge data from the North Carolina Administrative Office of the Courts. We defined “drug-related charges” as those related to the possession, manufacturing, or distribution of drugs. We did not include charges relating to driving while impaired. Table S1 includes a list of each criminal charge we included, the North Carolina statute to which the charge is tied, and its frequency in our data. We calculated county-level rates of drug-related charges per 1000 population ages ≥18 years. In a supplemental analysis, we restricted the drug-related charges to those for possession. Table S2 contains a list of these select charges.
We also used data from the US Census Bureau (percent population in poverty), Bureau of Labor Statistics (percent population unemployed), Eviction Lab (rate of evictions per 100 renter households), and County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (percent population food insecure and percent of adults uninsured) to construct county-level measures of social determinants of health. We chose these social determinants of health because they are enumerable and intervenable and because of their association with substance use disorders and increased policing. These social determinants of health can be modified through the implementation of evidence-based policies.
The UNC Institutional Review Board determined this research was exempt.
2. Analytic methods
For primary analyses, we included pre-COVID years (2016–2019). Using five spatial autoregressive models, we regressed county-level rates of drug-related charges (2017–2019) separately on each county-level social determinant of health (2016–2018). We allowed a one-year lag between the variables to ensure temporality between the independent and dependent variables (e.g., we regressed 2017 drug-related charge rates on each 2016 social determinant of health variable). We performed separate regression models for each social determinant of health variable, as opposed to multivariable analysis, because of lack of interpretability of a multivariable model in a non-causal context. We used spatial autoregressive models because of the spatial nature of our data. It is possible that rates of drug-related charges in a given county are affected by conditions in that county (direct effect) as well as conditions in nearby counties (indirect effects). Our spatial autoregressive models specifically accounted for spatial dependence of the independent variables (social determinant of health) and spatial correlation of the error terms. This allowed us to capture direct effects, or the effect of social determinants of health in a given county on rates of drug-related charges in that county; indirect, or “spillover”, effects in which rates of drug-related charges in a given county are affected social determinants of health in nearby counties; and total effects, or the sum of the direct and indirect effects. We used a weighting matrix of first-order neighbors in which neighbors are counties that share a common edge or vertex (Dubin, 2009). In a secondary analysis we assessed trends from 2016 to 2021.
3. Results
Annual county-level rates of drug-related charges per 1000 adults ranged from 12.6 to 101.6 in 2017, 13.9 to 99.6 in 2018, 9.6 to 66.0 in 2019, 13.1 to 41.2 in 2020, and 7.5 to 26.2 in 2021. In primary analyses, poverty, evictions, and food insecurity were not associated with the following-year rates of drug-related charges, while unemployment and uninsured status were associated with an increase in the rate of drug-related charges (Table 1). Specifically, a one percentage point increase in unemployment in a given county was associated with a 3.27 (95 % CI: 1.28, 5.26) percent increase in the rate of drug-related charges per 1000 adults in the following year. A one percentage point increase in the percent of the population without health insurance in a given county was associated with a 110.50 (95 % CI: 52.81, 168.18) percent increase in the rate of drug-related charges per 1000 adults in the following year. In supplemental analyses restricting to possession charges, we identified similar patterns with smaller effect sizes (Table S3).

Mean annual county-level drug-related charge rates decreased over the entire time period but decreased more quickly during the COVID-19 pandemic years. The mean rates of drug-related charges per 1000 adults in 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021 were 38.1, 36.2, 33.7, 27.5, and 29.4, respectively (Table S4). When we included the 2020 and 2021 data in spatial regression analyses, we found a small association between county-level poverty and subsequent year rates of drug-related charges such that a one percentage point increase in the percent of a population living in poverty in a given county was associated with a 0.61 (95 % CI: 0.18, 1.04) percent increase in the rate of drug-related charges per 1000 adults in the following year (Table S5). We also found that a one percentage point increase in the percent of the population without health insurance in a given county was associated with a 192.97 (95 % CI: 136.01, 248.34) percent increase in the rate of drug-related charges per 1000 adults in the following year.
4. Discussion
Given persistent increases in drug overdose deaths, there is a need to investigate common predecessors to these deaths, including involvement with the criminal-legal system. We assessed associations between county-level social determinants of health and rates of drug-related charges in North Carolina. We found an accelerating decrease in county rates of drug-related charges from 2017 to 2021. In primary analyses spanning 2016–2019, we also identified two county-level social determinants of health associated with subsequent year rates of drug-related charges: unemployment and uninsured status. These associations persisted in a supplementary analysis in which we only included drug possession charges. In secondary analyses spanning 2016–2021, uninsured status remained significantly associated with rates of drug-related charges.
In primary analyses, counties with higher unemployment and a higher percentage of their population without health insurance had higher rates of drug-related charges. Similar associations have been observed for other types of crime (He & Barkowski, 2020; Schleimer et al., 2022). While these analyses are purely descriptive and should be interpreted as such, we draw a few conclusions. Issues of SUD treatment affordability may be compounded for uninsured and unemployed individuals. It is possible that individuals with a SUD who cannot afford treatment may be arrested and criminally charged for possession, manufacturing, or distribution of drugs in lieu of receiving SUD treatment. If this is the case, it would indicate that individuals in need of treatment are criminally punished and often spend time incarcerated where they are unlikely to receive SUD treatment (Bronson et al., 2017; Hoover et al., 2023; Moore et al., 2020). We infer a need for alternative avenues to treatment in these counties, such as substance use diversion programs and drug-treatment courts (Lindquist-Grantz et al., 2021). Additionally, because not all people with drug-related charges are eligible for diversion programs and drug-treatment courts, expanded access to SUD treatment that is both geographically and financially accessible may be beneficial. While we do not assume that all people charged with drug-related offenses are in need of SUD treatment we note that results persisted when we only included drug possession charges and we posit that increased access to treatment would improve outcomes across the state and reduce demand for drugs, potentially limiting demand for drug distribution and manufacturing as well. Further, we infer a need for robust unemployment supports, such as investments in education and job training, expanded unemployment insurance (Pratap et al., 2021), and affordable health insurance options for those unemployed, to prevent a need to resort to illegal activities, including drug distribution and manufacturing, for economic stability (Nally et al., 2014; Phillips & Land, 2012). In secondary analyses that included years during the COVID-19 pandemic, we found that counties with a larger uninsured population continued to have higher rates of drug-related charges.
We note a few limitations. These analyses are descriptive and should not be interpreted causally. Additionally, data are aggregated at the county level. We cannot conclude from these analyses, for example, that uninsured individuals are more likely than their insured peers to be charged with a drug-related offense. Rather, counties with a higher percentage of their population without insurance have higher rates of drug-related charges. We note that our main analysis excludes 2020–2021 data because years during the COVID-19 pandemic were fundamentally different in many ways from the preceding years, and the COVID-19 pandemic did not necessarily mark the beginning of a persistent change in the trend in drug-related charges and the social determinants of health described in this paper. Finally, North Carolina has recently expanded Medicaid. We expect these results may change as Medicaid becomes available to more individuals.
5. Conclusion
These results are useful in describing the characteristics of North Carolina counties with higher rates of drug-related charges and providing a foundation for future causal analyses. Given racial disparities in engagement with the criminal-legal system, including disproportionate arrests of Black people for drug-related offenses, continued research and future interventions addressing drug-related charges is a health equity issue.
