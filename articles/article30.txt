Abstract
This review provides a narrative exploration of the literature on various social determinants of health that influence outcomes in axial Spondyloarthritis (axSpA). By using the PROGRESS-Plus framework (place of residence, race, occupation, gender/sex, religion, education, socioeconomic status, social capital, age), this review discusses how these factors have been studied and their impact on disease outcomes in axSpA. The findings suggest that various patient-level factors (e.g. female sex, blue-collar jobs, low educational level) and country-level factors (e.g. low-income countries) associate with worse health outcomes in axSpA. These insights highlight the importance of adopting a multifaceted and holistic approach, that also considers social determinants of health, when managing patients with axSpA. This work also identifies unmet needs in this area including the importance of thinking beyond just biological factors, when considering drivers of suboptimal outcomes in axSpA.

1. Introduction
Spondyloarthritis (SpA) represents a continuum of inflammatory diseases that typically affect the axial skeleton (axSpA) or the peripheral joints [1,2]. axSpA covers both patients who have developed structural damage in the sacroiliac joints visible on radiographs (radiographic axial spondyloarthritis [r-axSpA]), and those without structural damage on plain radiographs (non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis [nr-axSpA]) [3]. R-axSpA has historically been known as ankylosing spondylitis, but the nomenclature has recently been updated to reflect axSpA as the overall term of the disease, with both subtypes: r-axSpA and nr-axSpA [4]. AxSpA was traditionally considered a male-dominated disease, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1 for r-axSpA. However, with the earlier recognition of disease and the increasing number of patients fulfilling the nr-axSpA criteria [5] the sex distribution in this group has balanced to 1:1 [6].
AxSpA typically begins in early adulthood, usually during the third decade of life and almost exclusively before 45 years of age [7]. It is associated with significant impairments, physical limitations, and thus a reduced health related quality of life [8,9]. These impairments can affect patients throughout their lifetime, potentially limiting their ability to participate in various social roles, including work [10,11], and leading to increased costs for both the individual and society [12]. In addition to the disease itself, these outcomes are strongly influenced by external, social factors that can shape the disease's course and outcomes, particularly when disparities in these factors exist. Understanding these influences can improve disease management and enhance patient quality of life by identifying sources of health inequalities. In this narrative review, we explore the role of various social determinants of health (SoDH), as reported in the literature of axSpA and reflect on unmet needs and future research directions. A deeper understanding of these factors can support a more holistic patient assessment, leading to more targeted and effective management strategies. From a broader, societal perspective, these insights can help guide interventions to improve the care of people living with this disease and minimise health inequalities.
2. Social determinants of health: focus on PROGRESS-Plus factors
SDoH constitute a subgroup of contextual factors (CFs) - personal and external elements that influence health outcomes but are not directly part of the pathological or clinical manifestations of the disease [13]. These determinants encompass the social, economic, and environmental conditions that influence health outcomes [14]. That is all those conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, and the wider set of forces and systems shaping the conditions of daily life. To standardize the use of SDoH in healthcare research and to be incorporated in the design of new intervention studies and systematic reviews, key social determinants have been listed into the PROGRESS-Plus framework. PROGRESS refers to Place of residence (rural/urban/inner city), Race (ethnicity/culture/language) Occupation, Gender/sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status (SES), and Social capital.“Plus” includes personal characteristics that may lead to discrimination (e.g., age, disability), aspects of relationships (e.g., smoking parents, school exclusion), and time-dependent factors (e.g., leaving the hospital, respite care, or other situations where a person may be temporarily face disadvantages) [15]. This review has used the PROGRESS-Plus framework to guide a narrative search of the literature, to ensure focus on the most important SDoH in the context of axSpA. Among the factors in “Plus”, only “Age” will be discussed in detail given its status as one of the most extensively studied variables and its known impact on health outcomes in axSpA. Place of residence refers to (characteristics of) the location where a person lives such as level of industrialisation, population density (urban vs rural), cultural differences, economic wealth and level of development of a city, region or country, level of income inequality [15,16].
.Studies addressing this determinant investigated mainly the influence of the country-level macro-economic characteristics of patients' country of residence in multinational studies. A long-standing cohort of patients with r-axSpA from France, Belgium, and the Netherlands, the OASIS cohort, was among the first to demonstrate that country of residence significantly influenced work outcomes, with differences attributed to variations in social security systems [17]. This role of country of residence was later confirmed through two major multinational studies in SpA: the ASAS-COMOSPA [18] and the ASAS-perSpA study [19]. In two ancillary analyses from these studies [20,21], lower country-level healthcare expenditure (HCE) or gross domestic product (GDP) were consistently associated with poorer work participation outcomes (such as presenteeism, absenteeism, or employment). Additionally, in ASAS-perSpA, it was highlighted that the impact of sex on employment status varied across countries, with a greater discrepancy between males and females (who had higher odds of unemployment) in countries with lower socioeconomic development [21].
In terms of disease activity and physical function, while country macroeconomic indicators were not directly associated with physical function, patients in lower socioeconomic status (SES) countries as measured by HCE, GDP and human development index (HDI) consistently had higher disease activity levels (ASDAS β = 0.2–0.39) and were more likely to report active disease [22,23]. Paradoxically, higher fatigue levels were observed among patients living in wealthier countries, potentially indicating a sociocultural influence inherent to the multidimensional origin of fatigue [23]. When exploring variations in treatment uptake by country, analysis of the ASAS-COMOSPA study suggests a higher use of biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) for SpA in countries with higher health expenditures, although this association did not reach statistical significance. In line with this, csDMARD uptake was significantly lower in countries with higher health expenditures (OR 0.32 95 % CI 0.15 to 0.65) with similar trends seen with other country level SE characteristics [24]. This study suggested important residual variation across countries in bDMARD uptake, independent of traditional factors for bDMARD use such as clinical and country-level socioeconomic factors. Such insights are invaluable in understanding geographical variations in the outcomes of axSpA.
Race, refers to ethnic, and cultural background [15]. We recommend the addition of language to this component because many definitions of ethnicity include shared culture and shared language. This SDoH and can be assessed in multinational studies or studies in highly diverse populations. In axSpA, despite the limited number of studies and inconsistency in the findings, race has been shown to have some influence on health outcomes.
Based on data from the French inception cohort of axSpA (<3 years), DESIR, white race was associated with lower disease activity (BASDAI coef −8.9; MRI inflammation in the SI joints OR 0.4), and better health- related quality of life (ASQoL β −1.9) at baseline, but was not associated with radiographic sacroiliiitis, MRI inflammation in the spine, fatigue or BASFI [25]. It is worth noting that these findings were based on univariable analyses. However, at 5 years, white race (vs other ethnic groups) was associated with SI joint radiographic progression, mNY (β −1.03 95 %CI-1.75 to −0.30)) in analyses adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, blue-collar jobs, CRP status, BASDAI and csDMARDs use. This association was not observed with other imaging outcomes including inflammation (Spine MRI SPARCC, SI joint MRI SPARCC) or radiographic damage (mSASSS) [26].
Further studies found no link between race (Caucasian or white race) and fatigue in Spanish and French cohorts [[27], [28], [29]], nor with work outcomes in Brazilian, Canadian, Australian, Dutch, and French cohorts [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. In a study comparing ethnic backgrounds (Caucasian, African Brazilian, and others), African Brazilians reported slightly worse PGA, ASQoL, and MASES scores, although differences were minimal and analyses were not unadjusted for other variables [33].
These limited and inconsistent observations across cohorts—along with the minimal effect sizes in some comparisons—suggest that these racial differences in axSpA outcomes may be explained by other factors, such as (other) socioeconomic or cultural variables, that were not accounted for in these studies.
Occupation encompasses different situations including out of work, underemployment, informal workers, and (unsafe) working environments. It also refers to the type of work or profession a person engages in, typically as a means of earning a living [34]. It is often analyzed in terms of whether a patient is employed (or more accurately, holds a paid job), whether they work full-time or part-time, and/or the type of occupation. Many classifications exist on the nature or work a person is engaged in. It is important to note, however, that most classifications are country-specific, making them difficult to apply universally and thus limiting the comparability of studies. A frequently used classification categorizes workers into blue-collar (jobs that involve manual labor or skilled trades) and white-collar (jobs that are performed in an office or professional setting) [35].
Occupation has mainly been studied in relation to work outcomes and radiographic damage and to a lesser extent in relation to self-reported health. Regarding work outcomes, study findings are inconsistent: Pre-bDMARD-era studies, such as the OASIS cohort [17] and the nationwide Dutch Standard Diagnosis Register of Rheumatic Diseases registry [31], found that patients with blue-collar jobs had higher risks of sick leave (OR 2.9 95 % CI 1.1 to 7.5) [17] and labor force withdrawal due to disease (HR 2.3 95 % CI 1.5 to 3.4) [31]. Additionally, in an open-label, observational, multicenter study from Belgium, manual jobs were linked to higher presenteeism [36]. However, longitudinal data from the DESIR and OASIS cohorts showed no significant link between job type (irrespective of whether in full time or not) and work disability or adverse work outcomes [32,37].
Occupation may also influence disease activity outcomes. For instance, in the SPACE cohort, the impact of disease activity on quality of life was more significant for blue-collar workers than white-collar workers [38]. Similarly, in the DESIR cohort, smoking was associated with SI joint inflammation in blue-collar workers but not in white-collar workers (β 5.4 95 % CI 1.3 to 9.5 vs. β 0.2 95 % CI -1.1 to 1.6) [26]. No consistent association was found between employment status and fatigue and [28,30], though intense physical labor was associated with severe fatigue in the Spanish registry (OR 1.8 95 % CI 1.1 to 3.1) [27]. These results are not necessarily surprising. Such associations and their direction can be complex, especially in view of the inherent complexity of multifaceted variables like fatigue. For example, an employed patient (engaged in paid work) may experience more fatigue due to a combination of disease activity and the demands of their job, while patients with higher levels of fatigue might be more likely to be (become) unemployed, leading to the healthy worker effect. As one might expect, patients with chronic work disability tend to report lower health-related quality of life, likely due to the compounded effects of reduced income, loss of professional identity, limited social interactions, and the psychological burden associated with long-term inability to work. Moreover, these individuals often experienced cumulative more active disease and functional limitations, which adds to their challenges and negatively impacts their quality of life [17].
Studies focusing on occupation in the context of axSpA, such as the ones presented here, highlight the importance of adopting tailored management approaches, particularly for blue-collar workers, who may face heightened risks and challenges related to their occupational demands.
One of the most studied topics among SDoH is the impact of gender/sex on health outcomes, as differences between men and women result in varying health risks, disease incidence, and health service needs. In recent years, the differentiation and correct usage of the terms ‘sex’ and ‘gender’ have become increasingly important, and special attention needs to be given to this distinction, as these terms have often been used interchangeably in the literature. Sex refers to the biological differences between males and females, while gender, refers to the socially constructed roles and traits that society generally associates with each sex [15]. In this review we will use the term sex since this has been the most studied variable to date.
In general, data on the association between sex and work outcomes is consistent. The majority of recent studies have shown that males are more likely to be employed than females, with higher likelihood of employment (1.9–2.5 times greater) [20,21,39]. Nevertheless, this is in line with the general population, as shown by a Dutch study, where the standardized employment ratios (relative chance of patients with r-axSpA having a paid job compared with controls) was very similar in male and female patients (0.84 vs 0.80). The adjusted absolute employment rates for patients and controls were 73 % vs 86 % for males and 62 % vs 78 % for females [40].
In terms of studies looking specifically at work disability, some studies show no differences by sex [31,32,37], while others suggest worse outcome for females. For instance, in a study from the USA the standardized employment rate in women (axSpA vs population) was 3.2 (95 % CI: 2.1 to 4.8) whereas for men this was 2.1 (95 % CI: 1. 6 to 2.7) [34].
When it comes to presenteeism and sick leave, men consistently have better outcomes with male sex associated with shorter sick leave durations [[41], [42], [43], [44]] although not always statistically significant [21,30,39,45]. For example, in the DESIR cohort male sex was independently associated with a lower hazard of sick leave (HR 0.4 95 % CI 0.2 to 0.9) [44], or in a longitudinal analysis from a cohort of patients treated with Infliximab, male vs female sex was negatively associated with the length of sick leave (β −1.3 95 % CI -2.4 to −0.2) [41]. In the same study, an open label trial involving patients with paid work who were on a stable dose of infliximab over a 96-week period, found no association with presenteeism, but the impact of presenteeism (the main predictor of sick leave), as well as other variables like BASDAI and BASFI, on the length of sick leave over time, was stronger in female compared to male patients. After six months, the predicted sick leave for patients with presenteeism of 0 and 7 (where 0 = normal productivity and 10 = completely affected productivity) was 0.3 and 4.0 days, respectively for males, and 1.0 and 14.5 days, respectively, for females [41].
In general, the existing data suggest that female sex is generally associated worse health outcomes, though there are some exceptions. Female sex is consistently and independently associated with higher disease activity scores, such as BASDAI and ASDAS [22,23,[46], [47], [48]] although for ASDAS several studies found no association with sex [25,47,48]. A key difference between these two instruments is the fact that BASDAI is a fully self-reported instrument that also includes fatigue and enthesitis, with known differences between men and women [25,48]. In contrast, ASDAS incorporates objective measures of inflammation, such as CRP levels. This distinction may explain why some studies report no difference in ASDAS scores between men and women with axSpA, suggesting that ASDAS is more ‘stable’ and less influenced by sex [48] making it the preferred instrument for measuring inflammation in axSpA [49].
In terms of other outcomes, in studies exploring associations with physical function, female sex shows higher (worse) BASFI [23,50], or higher likelihood to increase by 4 or more units the BASFI in 2 years (OR 1.9) [51] or no association at all [25,47]. Other self-reported outcomes associated with female sex are fatigue [23,25,[27], [28], [29]] and health related quality of life/ functioning and health [25,47,50], including worse scores on the SpA-specific ASAS HI [23,52] although not all studies found this association [53].
What is particularly interesting in the existing literature is that, when assessing more objective outcomes, like MRI inflammation, there appears to be a negative association with female sex (MRI-SIJ OR 0.5 and MRI-spine OR 0.5) [25]. Aligned with these findings, radiographic SI damage, also tends to be lower in female sex: most studies in early SpA found less radiographic sacroiliitis (i.e. OR for SIJ 0.6 95 %CI 0.4 to 0.9) [25,46,47,54],or at least numerical differences although not always statistically significant [25]. The latter may relate to the duration of follow-up. For example, in the DESIR cohort at 5 years, no association between radiographic damage (spine or SIJ) and sex was observed [25]. However, in the 10-year analysis, male sex was associated with a higher likelihood of becoming mNY+ over time [54]. One of the most consistent findings relates in fact to spinal structural damage, whereby male sex is associated with higher mSASSS scores over time [47]. This has been nicely demonstrated in a longitudinal 12-year analysis of the OASIS cohort where male sex (vs female) was significantly linked to higher mSASSS progression over time (β = 8.2 95 % CI 4.4–12.1) [47].
Religion can be defined as a system of beliefs, practices, and moral codes that connect individuals to the sacred or transcendent [55]. It often provides explanations about existence, purpose, and morality while shaping individual and collective identities. It has been shown to contribute to inequities via differential access to health services which can be limited for a subgroup of the population due of their religious affiliation (or lack of religion) [56]. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have looked specifically at the effect of religion on axSpA outcomes so this remains a largely under-explored area.
For example, certain religious groups may have health-related beliefs or practices that influence how they manage their condition, such as preferences for specific treatments or the avoidance of others. These beliefs, combined with ethnic and cultural differences, could contribute to variations in how axSpA is manifested and managed across different populations. Despite these potential intersections between religion, ethnicity, and axSpA outcomes, this area remains largely unexplored and warrants further investigation to better understand its impact.
Education can be defined as the process of acquiring knowledge, skills, values, and attitudes. Formal education takes place within a structured institutional framework, such as public schools, and follows a set curriculum. Non-formal education, while also structured, occurs outside the formal schooling system, whereas informal education involves unstructured learning that happens through daily experiences. Although the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) categorizes formal education into 8 levels [57], studies to date have largely used three broad categories: primary, secondary, and tertiary (or higher) education.
Education is a key social determinant of health, directly and indirectly influencing lifestyle choices, health knowledge, treatment adherence, and overall well-being [58]. Higher education level has been associated with healthier behaviors, such as physical activity and less smoking, and better health outcomes [59]. Whether this is a direct effect of education or whether other parameters such as health literacy act as mediators of these associations, is an area that deserves more attention. The challenge is that health literacy has not been a routinely collected variable in epidemiological research, limiting its study and at the same time, highlighting an unmet need.
Health literacy is a determinant of health, and according to the World Health Organization, represents the personal knowledge and competencies that accumulate through daily activities, social interactions and across generations. It is defined as the ability to access, a understand, appraise and use information and services in ways that promote and maintain good health and well-being [60]. Health literacy represents an “actionable SDoH” and therefore potentially modifiable. By supporting people with limitations in health literacy, especially in lower-educated populations, we could mitigate some of the adverse outcomes observed in this group, promoting better quality of life, improved functioning, and more effective disease management. Further research in axSpA is needed to fully demonstrate this potential impact. However, similar effects have already been observed in other rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis [61].
While some studies in axSpA have consistently found that lower education levels were independently associated with poorer work participation outcomes, such as presenteeism, absenteeism and employment status [21,44,62] or higher number of sick days [30], some other studies did not observe such association [20,45]. Lower education was also associated with higher work disability; for instance, in the Dutch Standard Diagnosis Register of Rheumatic Diseases, patients with 12 or fewer years of formal education had an OR of 3.2 (95 %CI 1.6 to 6.5) for work disability [31]. Similarly, in the DESIR cohort, lower education was linked to work disability, although this was only significant in the model adjusted for BASMI. When disease activity was included, the significance of education diminished, with the addition of ASDAS resulting in a 30 % reduction in the HR for education [32]. This study suggested that the effect of education on work disability may be mediated by disease activity. On the other hand, results from the OASIS cohort showed no significant associations between education and work disability or adverse work outcomes [37].
Important factor to consider is the association between low education and more physically demanding jobs (blue-collar) or lifestyle. Individuals with lower education levels are more likely to be employed in occupations involving manual labor or other physically demanding tasks. Although speculative, it may be that these jobs often offer less flexibility in terms of sick leave, fewer work accommodations, and limited opportunities for career advancement or transitioning to less physically demanding roles [26]. Concerning lifestyle, smoking, a habit more prevalent among lower-educated individuals and blue-collar workers [63,64], can amplify disease progression, as suggested by studies demonstrating more inflammation and radiographic damage in smoking lower educated and smoking blue collar workers [26,65,66].
In the literature of axSpA, lower education is consistently associated with worse health status. For example, in the ASAS-COMOSPA study, patients with only primary education had higher ASDAS (β 0.3 95 %CI 0.2 to 0.4), higher odds of having ASDAS ≥2.1 (OR 1.7 95 %CI 1.3 to 2.2) and a higher BASFI score (β 0.6 95 %CI 0.3 to 0. 8) compared with patients with university education [22]. Same results were found in the ASAS-perSpA study, where an additional association was found with worse overall functioning and health (ASAS-HI) [23]. In the DESIR cohort, a BASFI increase by at least 4 units at 2 years was also associated with lower education (no college OR 3.0 95 %CI 1.8 to 5.0) [51].
Education has also been studied in terms of its association with other, patient reported (patient perceived) outcomes including fatigue, with results generally suggesting that patients with lower education have higher odds of experiencing significant fatigue (>5 points on a scale from 0 to 10) [[27], [28], [29]]. In contrast, data from the multinational ASAS-perSpA study, in models adjusted primarily for disease activity as well as other predictors and confounders, showed no such association [23]. Furthermore, the limited data available regarding quality of life did not reveal any association with education [67].
Socioeconomic status (SES) describes people based on their social class (income, profession) or the wealth of the country in which they live (high income country). SES is a critical determinant of health that influences various aspects of an individual's well-being, like the access to resources, health behaviors, education, living conditions, social support, and intergenerational health effects [68]. Country level SES and other related SDoH have already been addressed in relevant sections higher up. Here, we will therefore exclusively refer to the individual level SES (mainly income), acknowledging that data are limited on this topic.
An intriguing paradox, though, deserves some attention and reminds of the associations observed with fatigue (higher) in wealthier countries. In the Korean CASCO cohort, patients with higher income demonstrated worse ASAS-HI scores (β = −1.1, p = 0.013) [53]. This counterintuitive finding suggests that higher SES, which is typically associated with better health outcomes, may not necessarily translate to improved health-related quality of life or functioning in axSpA patients. The interpretation of these findings is subject to speculation and demands more research, potentially with adoption of mixed-methodologies, to understand observed associations. However, potential explanations could include heightened expectations of health or functional abilities among wealthier individuals, greater work-related stress, or other unmeasured factors specific to this population.
Social capital refers to social relationships and networks, and encompasses interpersonal trust within a community, civic engagement, mutual assistance, and political connections, all of which can facilitate access to services [69]. Marital status is often used as a surrogate to measure social capital as (partially) reflects an individual's social relationships and support networks. In health contexts, marital status affects emotional support, coping strategies, health behaviors, mental health, and economic stability, which in turn can improve healthcare access. Married individuals often experience greater emotional support, healthier behaviors, and better mental health outcomes [70]. Generally, marital status across various axSpA studies has not been found to be associated with work related outcomes such as presenteeism, sick leave [30] or work disability [11,31,44].
In relation to disease activity, little research has been conducted. Data from the international ASAS perSpA study showed that being divorced or widowed (compared to being married) was associated with higher odds of active disease measured by ASDAS (OR 1.7 95 %CI 1.3 to 2.3). Although when studied as a continuous variable the effect size was small, divorced or widowed patients had higher ASDAS scores, while single individuals had higher BASFI scores compared to married patients (with β 0.2 95 %CI 0.1 to 0.3) and β 0.2 95 %CI 0.0 to 0.4, respectively) [23].
Interestingly, in an univariable analysis from the Spanish register, married patients had a higher odds for severe fatigue (OR 1.56 95 %CI 1.1 to 2.19) [27]. However, in multivariable analyses across different populations and cultural contexts, no association between marital status and fatigue was found [23,28,29,71]. Additionally, data from the DESIR cohort showed that neither marital status nor parental status was associated with robust measures of inflammation, namely sacroiliac joint inflammation as seen on MRI [26].
An important point to consider is that while marital status is often used as a surrogate for social capital, it may not fully capture the complexity of an individual's support network. Marriage can provide both positive and negative forms of support, depending on the quality of the relationship. A supportive spouse can significantly enhance an individual's ability to cope with health challenges, whereas an unsupportive or dysfunctional marriage may exacerbate stress and hinder disease management. Furthermore, individuals who are not married may still have strong, reliable sources of support through family, friends, or community networks, which may contribute equally to their social capital. Therefore, relying solely on marital status as a measure of social support overlooks the multifaceted nature of social capital and its varying impact on health outcomes.
Age as a SDoH, creates divisions and hierarchies within society, shaping social position based on age. Ageism leads to various harms, disadvantages, and injustices, including age-related health inequities and poorer health outcomes [72]. While age reflects the impact of the aging process on health outcomes, its interpretation can be challenging due to potential confounding with disease duration. For instance, in the Dutch cohort of r-axSpA, in patients of working age, each additional year of age increased the odds of work disability by 9 % (OR 1.09 95 % CI 1.06 to 1.13) [31], and was linked to lower employment among those with lower education levels (IRR 0.98 95 % CI 0.97 to 0.99) [40]. This aligns with studies suggesting that older patients are generally less likely to be employed than younger ones [37,39],; however, two multinational studies found no association between age and employment [20,21], and the DESIR cohort, an inception cohort of axSpA, found no link between age and work disability [11].
Regarding presenteeism or sick leave, most of the studies showed no association with age [20,21,30,39,41,45,73], with the exception of one analysis from the DESIR cohort, where age was associated with a higher hazard of sick leave (HR 1.1 95 %CI 1.0 to 1.1) in models also adjusting for sex, education, smoking status, treatment and disease activity (ASDAS), functional status (BASFI), or mobility (BASMI), the latter three included in separate models [44].
For disease activity and physical function, the impact of age was modest, with only small (or no) changes observed on ASDAS and BASFI scores [22,23]. An analysis from DESIR, however, found that age (>33 years old) was associated with an increased likelihood (OR 2.3 95 % CI 1.4 to 3.8) of functional decline, defined as a 4-point increase in BASFI over two years (model adjusted for sex, education, smoking status, disease activity and fulfillment of ASAS criteria) [51].
Age was minimally [25,47,50] or not associated with health related quality of life [38] or the ASAS-HI [23,52,53]. Interestingly, data from the Spanish register suggested some (minimal) association between fatigue with age [27] but several national and international cohorts found no such link [23,28,29,71].
Regarding imaging, age was not consistently associated with MRI-detected inflammation [25,26], though it associated slightly negatively with radiographic damage in the sacroiliac joints (OR 0.96) but positively with spine damage (β = 0.6 95 % CI 0.4 to 0.7), the latter likely due to its association with disease duration [25,47].

3. Methodological considerations
The study of SDoH to date has been both exciting and challenging, rightly placing these factors firmly on the candidate list for important and relevant factors influencing disease outcomes. Traditional epidemiological research is not necessarily appropriate for the study of these factors, which demand more detail and complex analysis. Specifically, considerations need to be given to the study of SDoH as independent predictors of outcomes, and/or confounders or effect modifiers, altering the impact of other predictors. There are plenty of examples in the existing literature that directly or indirectly raise these issues. For example, in the SPACE cohort, although sex and job type independently affected quality of life, the impact of disease activity on health-related quality of life (measured by PCS from the SF-36) was lower for females (β = −7.7 95 % CI –9.9 to −5.5) than for males (β = −11.0 95 % CI –13.7 to −8.4), and in white-collar workers (β = −9.6 95 %CI –12.3 to −7.0) than in blue-collar workers (β = −15.6 95 % CI –23.0 to −8.3) [38]. Similarly, a 6-year longitudinal analysis from the OASIS cohort found that sex was not associated with overall risk of sick leave, but in lower educated males disease activity predicted extended sick leave [43].
Further, mediation is critical consideration. In simple terms, refers to when an independent variable (e.g., treatment or exposure) affects a dependent variable (e.g., health outcome) indirectly through another factor (mediator). For instance, a DESIR cohort analysis showed that patients transitioning from very high to inactive disease within three years were more likely to be male (OR = 7.1 95 % CI 1.6 to 32.3), highly educated (OR = 9.4 95 % CI 1.4 to 63.4), and have peripheral joint involvement (OR = 6.2 95 % CI 1.3 to 31.3). They also had higher TNFi use (HR = 4.5 95 % CI 3.5 to 5.9), suggesting better treatment access or adherence among educated men, or possibly less severe disease [74]. From a methodological perspective, even when considering longitudinal data, these complex relationships can remain difficult to disentangle, calling for different analytical approaches including mediation analyses or structural equation modelling to better understand the underlying factors and their interconnections. As an example of mediation analysis, the ASAS-perSpA study found that the lower use of advanced therapies (b/tsDMARDs) partially mediated the association between lower GDP countries and higher ASDAS, with 7 % of the difference (0.02 of 0.34 ASDAS units) attributed to reduced medication access [23].
Lastly, it is also important to assess the influence of measurement properties. This is exemplified in a study by our group aimed at developing thresholds for presenteeism instruments to identify patients who perceive themselves as being in an unacceptable work state. Specifically, two out of the four presenteeism instruments analyzed demonstrated differences in performance of the newly developed cut-offs between blue- and white-collar workers. This suggests that blue-collar workers may be at risk of being overestimated as dissatisfied with their work status when these thresholds are applied, which affects the instrument's discrimination [75].
4. Unmet needs
While it is crucial to focus on SDoH alongside biological drivers of disease when studying the factors that influence outcomes in axSpA (Fig. 2), there are several key unmet need that must be addressed. The adoption of a bio-psychosocial approach in the study of disease has long been encouraged, but not often appropriately applied. Relying on crudely measured SDoH variables from cohort and other epidemiological studies or trial data is simply not adequate to properly appreciate their role on outcomes. For instance, the effect of aging on work outcomes highlights the need for more nuanced interpretations. Tools commonly used to assess presenteeism in axSpA typically range from worst-case scenarios to idealized “perfect scores.” However, even healthy individuals without rheumatic or other chronic diseases may fail to achieve perfect scores, particularly with advancing age. Ignoring the natural aging process in such assessments risks overestimating the disease's impact on presenteeism unless proper age adjustments are made [76].

It is also important to mention that a major limitation in pooling data together from across studies, as highlighted in this review, is the lack of standardization in measuring SDoH. While the PROGRESS-Plus framework outlines essential factors to assess in trials, there is no consensus on how to measure these variables, resulting in considerable variation. In traditional epidemiological research, these data are often assessed at a basic level, without the depth needed for more detailed analyses. The absence of such specificity limits our understanding of the underlying processes and makes it difficult to draw precise, comparable conclusions. This challenge is particularly evident in the measurement of educational level, where differing approaches contribute to inconsistencies across studies.
Before concluding, two important points on the research with SDoH need to be raised: First, we must be cautious not to stigmatize groups based on socioeconomic variables, as these are often surrogates for biological and/or behavioral mechanisms that are not yet fully understood. These mechanisms may be rooted either in the individual or in the system. Second, while the inequities are large and concerning, we should recognize that some choices are made consciously (e.g., based on religious or personal convictions) and are not necessarily unfair. In such cases, these choices are rooted in free will and should not be classified as inequities.
5. Future research
Looking ahead, further research is needed to address gaps in our understanding of the role of sex and gender in axSpA outcomes. Exploring both, biological and sociocultural factors can shed light on the etiology of observed differences. When undertaking such research, though, one has to acknowledge that the findings can be subject to speculation and unless all parameters are measured and accounted for, true relationships and causality can be challenging and difficult to establish. For example, a socially vulnerable individual may be subject to chronic stress, with consequences on several biological pathways and therefore outcomes.
The lack of standardization in the measurement of SDoH, as previously mentioned, highlights the need for further research in this area. Such research could aim to define a core set of SDoH that would be suitable for consistent collection in epidemiological studies.
Research into the underlying mechanism that linking SDoH to health outcomes is also crutial. As mentioned, factors such as biological influences (sex, stress), methodological challenges (e.g., confounding variables affecting outcomes), and the interactions between the individual and the health care system (e.g., health literacy) all play significant roles and warrant further investigation.
Another important area for future research is the potential need for stratification in clinical trials for variables such as sex or occupation (e.g., blue-collar versus white-collar workers). Failing to account for these confounding factors can impact on the study results, underscoring the importance of relevant adjustments in trial design. In clinical practice, better understanding of the role of SDoH can guide more targeted management and inform public health actions and health policies. The latter may involve strategies to enhance for example health literacy and therefore access to health information and services [77], ultimately reducing disparities.
In summary, this review discusses key SDoH according to the PROGRESS-Plus framework bringing to light insights from key studies in axSpA. Overall, the most frequently studied SDoH in epidemiological research are sex, occupation, education and country of residency. These studies consistently report worse outcomes among individuals living in low-income countries, with lower education levels, blue-collar jobs, and different outcomes based on sex: typically worse subjective outcomes in females and more severe objective outcomes in males. While the remaining determinants have been explored to varying extents, further studies are needed to fully appreciate their impact on axSpA outcomes. A comprehensive understanding of these aspects will not only contribute to enhancing patient outcomes but also will enable a more personalized and holistic approach to the management of individuals living with axSpA.
